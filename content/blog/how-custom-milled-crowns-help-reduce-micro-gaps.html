Introduction

Many patients who have had a dental crown fitted at some point wonder whether it is sitting correctly against the tooth. You may have noticed slight sensitivity around the crown margin, or perhaps your dentist has mentioned the need for a replacement during a routine check-up. These concerns are more common than you might think, and they are often linked to a concept known as micro-gaps — tiny spaces that can form between a crown and the underlying tooth structure.

Understanding how custom-milled crowns help reduce micro-gaps is increasingly relevant as dental technology advances. More patients are researching crown materials and fabrication methods before committing to treatment, and rightly so. Precision in crown fitting has a meaningful impact on long-term oral health outcomes.

This article explains what micro-gaps are, how they form, and why custom-milled crowns — produced using CAD/CAM (computer-aided design and manufacturing) technology — may offer improved marginal fit compared with some traditionally fabricated restorations. As always, suitability for any crown treatment depends on an individual clinical assessment.

What Are Micro-Gaps in Dental Crowns and How Do Custom-Milled Crowns Help?

Micro-gaps are tiny spaces that can form at the junction between a dental crown and the prepared tooth surface. Custom-milled crowns, produced using precise CAD/CAM technology, are digitally designed and machined to closely match the individual tooth's dimensions, which may help achieve a more accurate marginal fit and reduce the risk of micro-gaps forming over time.

What Are Micro-Gaps and Why Do They Matter?

A dental crown is a cap-like restoration placed over a damaged, weakened, or heavily restored tooth. For a crown to function well and protect the underlying tooth, it must fit snugly at the margin — the edge where the crown meets the prepared tooth structure at or near the gumline.

A micro-gap is a small, often imperceptible space at this junction. While it may seem insignificant, even a minute gap can create pathways for oral bacteria, saliva, and food debris to enter the space between the crown and the tooth. Over time, this can contribute to:

  • Secondary decay developing beneath the crown
  • Sensitivity to temperature or pressure
  • Cement washout, where the bonding material gradually dissolves
  • Gum inflammation around the crown margin
  • Crown loosening if the underlying tooth structure deteriorates

Micro-gaps are not always visible or symptomatic in the early stages, which is why regular dental check-ups are important for patients with crowns. Dentists can assess the integrity of crown margins using clinical examination and dental radiographs. Early identification of marginal issues can allow for timely intervention, potentially avoiding more complex treatment later.

How Crowns Are Traditionally Made Versus Custom-Milled

To understand how custom-milled crowns may help reduce micro-gaps, it helps to appreciate the difference in fabrication methods.

Traditional Laboratory-Fabricated Crowns

Traditionally, crowns are made by taking a physical impression of the prepared tooth using dental putty or impression material. This impression is sent to a dental laboratory, where a technician casts a plaster model and constructs the crown by hand — most commonly from porcelain-fused-to-metal (PFM) or full ceramic materials. The process relies significantly on manual skill and the accuracy of the physical impression.

Whilst skilled dental technicians produce excellent results, the multi-step nature of the traditional process introduces variables at each stage — from impression accuracy, to model preparation, to hand-layering of ceramic — that may influence the final marginal fit.

Custom-Milled CAD/CAM Crowns

Custom-milled crowns use digital scanning technology to capture a precise three-dimensional image of the prepared tooth. This digital impression is then processed using specialist software, which designs the crown to very close tolerances. The design file is sent to a milling machine that carves the crown from a solid block of ceramic or composite material.

This digital workflow reduces the number of manual steps involved and allows for highly consistent dimensional accuracy. The result is a crown that is custom-designed to the exact contours of an individual patient's prepared tooth, potentially improving marginal adaptation and reducing micro-gaps.

The Clinical Science Behind Marginal Fit

The term marginal fit refers to how closely and accurately a crown's edge conforms to the finish line of the prepared tooth — the line where the dentist has shaped the tooth to receive the crown. Achieving optimal marginal fit is one of the most technically demanding aspects of restorative dentistry.

Clinically, acceptable marginal discrepancy (the measurable gap at the crown margin) has been debated extensively in dental research. Studies generally suggest that smaller marginal gaps are associated with better long-term clinical outcomes, including reduced secondary decay and improved crown longevity.

CAD/CAM technology allows crown margins to be designed digitally with a high level of precision. Modern milling machines can produce ceramic restorations with marginal gaps that are clinically considered to be within acceptable ranges. Importantly, the fit also depends on:

  • The quality of the digital scan taken at the chairside
  • The condition of the prepared tooth and surrounding gum tissue
  • The material chosen for milling — different ceramics have different properties
  • The skill and experience of the treating dentist in tooth preparation

It is also worth noting that the quality of the cement or bonding agent used to place the crown, and the technique used during cementation, play a role in minimising micro-gaps at the final stage of treatment.

Benefits of Improved Marginal Fit in Custom-Milled Crowns

When custom-milled crowns achieve a closer marginal fit, several potential oral health benefits may follow. It is important to note that no restoration can be guaranteed to perform perfectly indefinitely — outcomes depend on individual factors including oral hygiene, diet, and the health of the underlying tooth.

That said, a well-fitting crown with reduced micro-gaps may:

  • Limit bacterial ingress beneath the crown, reducing the risk of secondary caries
  • Improve the longevity of the cement seal, keeping the crown stable
  • Reduce post-placement sensitivity by minimising exposed dentinal tubules at the margin
  • Support healthier gum tissue around the crown margin, as tight-fitting edges are generally easier to clean effectively
  • Reduce the need for premature crown replacement, which may be necessary when margins fail

These potential advantages are one reason why CAD/CAM technology has become increasingly adopted in modern dental practices across the UK. Same-day crown fabrication, in clinics equipped with chairside milling units, also reduces the need for temporary crowns and multiple appointments — a practical benefit for many patients.

Materials Used in Custom-Milled Crowns

The material from which a milled crown is fabricated also influences its properties, including aesthetics, strength, and compatibility with tooth structure.

Zirconia

Zirconia is a highly durable ceramic material widely used for milled crowns. It offers excellent strength and is particularly suited to posterior (back) teeth that experience greater biting forces. High-translucency zirconia has improved its aesthetic properties in recent years, making it suitable for visible front teeth as well.

Lithium Disilicate

Lithium disilicate (commonly known by the trade name e.max) is a glass-ceramic material offering a good balance of strength and natural aesthetics. It is widely used for anterior crowns and is prized for its lifelike translucency.

Composite Resin Blocks

Some chairside milling systems use resin-ceramic composite blocks. These are generally less durable than full-ceramic options but can be appropriate in certain clinical situations.

Your dentist will recommend a material based on the location of the tooth, your bite, aesthetic goals, and the clinical condition of the tooth — all of which require a thorough examination to assess properly.

When You Might Notice a Problem With an Existing Crown

Not all crown margin issues cause obvious symptoms, but there are situations where it may be worth discussing your crown with your dentist. These include:

  • Persistent sensitivity around a crowned tooth, particularly to cold or sweet foods
  • A change in the feel of the crown when biting or closing your teeth together
  • Visible discolouration or a dark line at the crown margin near the gumline
  • Recurring gum soreness or bleeding around a specific tooth
  • A crown that feels loose or slightly mobile
  • An unusual taste around the area of the crown

These signs do not automatically indicate a serious problem, but they are worth reporting to your dental team so that the crown margin can be assessed. Early assessment can help identify whether the crown requires monitoring, adjustment, or replacement.

When Professional Dental Assessment May Be Needed

If you have an existing crown or are considering one, there are several circumstances where seeking professional dental assessment is advisable:

  • Before any crown treatment: An examination, including X-rays, helps assess the health of the underlying tooth and surrounding bone, confirming whether a crown is the most appropriate restoration.
  • If sensitivity develops: Sensitivity around a crowned tooth that persists beyond the first few weeks following placement warrants review.
  • If you notice gum changes: Redness, swelling, or recession around a crown margin should be evaluated, as this can sometimes indicate a marginal issue or localised gum condition.
  • At routine check-ups: Crowns should be assessed at regular dental examinations even when no symptoms are present. Radiographs can detect secondary decay beneath a crown before it becomes symptomatic.
  • If the crown chips or cracks: Damaged ceramic crowns can compromise the marginal seal and may require repair or replacement.

Dental symptoms and treatment options should always be assessed individually during a clinical examination.

Oral Hygiene and Prevention Around Crown Margins

Even the most precisely fitted crown requires consistent oral hygiene to remain in good condition. The margin between a crown and the tooth is a vulnerable area where plaque can accumulate if cleaning is not thorough.

Practical oral hygiene tips for crowned teeth:

  • Brush twice daily using a fluoride toothpaste, paying attention to the gumline around the crown
  • Floss daily around crowned teeth — use dental floss or an interdental brush to clean the crown margin carefully
  • Consider a water flosser as a supplementary tool to clean around crown margins, particularly if you have multiple restorations
  • Avoid very sticky or extremely hard foods that could stress the crown or potentially dislodge it
  • Attend regular dental check-ups and hygienist appointments to allow professional monitoring of crown margins and adjacent gum tissue
  • Wear a nightguard if you grind your teeth — bruxism places significant forces on crowns and can increase the risk of fracture or marginal failure over time

Good home care, combined with regular professional assessment, supports the long-term health of crowned teeth and the surrounding gum tissue.

Frequently Asked Questions

What causes micro-gaps to form in dental crowns?

Micro-gaps at crown margins can arise from several factors. Inaccuracies in the physical impression of the tooth, variations during laboratory fabrication, cement dissolution over time, and changes to the underlying tooth structure can all contribute. In some cases, minor discrepancies in fit are present from the time of placement. Digital scanning and CAD/CAM milling aim to reduce the dimensional variability in the fabrication process, though the clinical outcome also depends on tooth preparation technique, material choice, and ongoing oral care.

Are custom-milled crowns better than traditional crowns?

Custom-milled crowns offer certain advantages in terms of the precision of digital fabrication and consistency of dimensional accuracy. However, "better" depends entirely on the individual clinical situation. Traditional laboratory-fabricated crowns, produced by skilled technicians, can also achieve excellent results. Your dentist will consider the specific needs of your tooth, your bite, aesthetic requirements, and other factors to recommend the most appropriate type of crown for you. A clinical assessment is necessary before any treatment recommendation can be made.

How long do custom-milled crowns typically last?

The longevity of any dental crown depends on multiple factors, including the material used, the clinical condition of the underlying tooth, the patient's bite and habits (such as teeth grinding), and the quality of daily oral hygiene. Custom-milled ceramic crowns can be durable restorations, but no specific lifespan can be guaranteed. Regular dental examinations allow your dentist to monitor the condition of your crown and identify any early signs of wear, damage, or marginal deterioration that may require attention.

Can I feel a micro-gap in my crown?

Micro-gaps are generally very small and are not typically detectable by feel alone. However, if a gap is present and has allowed bacteria to penetrate, you may notice symptoms such as sensitivity to cold, sweetness, or pressure around the crowned tooth, or occasional mild discomfort at the gumline. Some patients with marginal issues remain asymptomatic for a period. This is why regular dental check-ups — including radiographic examination of crowned teeth — are important for detecting problems that may not yet be causing noticeable symptoms.

Is same-day crown treatment with CAD/CAM technology reliable?

Chairside CAD/CAM systems that mill crowns within a single appointment have become increasingly common and are used successfully in many dental practices. They offer the convenience of completing treatment in one visit and eliminate the need for a temporary crown. The reliability of same-day milled crowns depends on the quality of the digital scan, the milling equipment used, the material selected, and the clinical expertise of the treating dentist. As with all dental treatment, an individual consultation is required to assess whether this approach is suitable for a specific tooth and clinical situation.

Do I need a crown if my tooth is only slightly damaged?

Not every damaged or weakened tooth requires a crown. Depending on the extent of the damage, your dentist may recommend alternative restorations such as an inlay, onlay, or composite filling. A crown is typically considered when a tooth has significant structural loss, has undergone root canal treatment, or requires protection from further fracture. The appropriate restoration can only be determined following a thorough clinical examination, which may include dental X-rays to assess the extent of any underlying damage or decay.

Conclusion

Understanding how custom-milled crowns help reduce micro-gaps offers valuable insight for anyone considering crown treatment or seeking to understand an existing restoration. Precision at the crown margin is a clinically meaningful aspect of restorative dentistry — one that influences the long-term health of the tooth, the surrounding gum tissue, and the durability of the restoration itself.

CAD/CAM technology represents a significant development in this area, enabling crowns to be digitally designed and milled to close tolerances that may improve marginal adaptation. However, it is important to remember that fabrication technology is one factor among many — including tooth preparation technique, material selection, cementation, and patient oral hygiene — that together determine how well a crown performs over time.
