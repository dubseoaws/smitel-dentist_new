Introduction

Having a dental crown placed is one of the most common restorative procedures, yet many patients leave their dentist feeling uncertain about their bite — even after a careful fitting adjustment. If your dental crown still feels too high after an adjustment, you are not alone. This is one of the most frequently searched concerns among patients who have recently received a new crown.

A crown that feels elevated, uncomfortable, or slightly "off" when biting down can cause significant day-to-day discomfort. Understanding why this happens, what your body is telling you, and when to return for further assessment can make a meaningful difference to your recovery and confidence in your treatment.

In this article, we explore the dental science behind bite adjustment after crown placement, the reasons a crown may continue to feel high, common associated symptoms, and when seeking further professional dental advice would be appropriate. Our aim is to provide clear, balanced information to help you make sense of your experience.

Can a Crown Feel Too High Even After an Adjustment?

Yes, a dental crown can feel too high even after an adjustment. This may occur because the bite is evaluated with anaesthetic still active, making accurate sensation difficult. Once the numbness wears off, minor height discrepancies may become more noticeable. Sensitivity, muscle awareness, and occlusal contact all influence how a crown feels during biting and chewing.

Why Does a Crown Feel High After Placement?

When a dental crown is fitted, your dentist will assess and adjust your bite using articulating paper — a thin, coloured paper that marks the points where your upper and lower teeth make contact. The aim is to achieve even, balanced contact across all teeth.

However, several factors can make this process imprecise, particularly on the day of fitting:

  • Local anaesthetic numbs the surrounding tissues, which significantly reduces your ability to feel subtle bite differences. What feels acceptable under anaesthetic may feel noticeably high once sensation returns.
  • Temporary crowns alter muscle memory slightly over the period they are worn. When the permanent crown is placed, your jaw muscles may need time to adapt to the new contact point.
  • Crown material also plays a role. Porcelain, zirconia, and metal crowns each have slightly different surface properties that affect how forces distribute across the bite.
  • Swelling and sensitivity in the surrounding gum tissue immediately after treatment can temporarily change how contact feels between teeth.

It is worth noting that even a height difference of a fraction of a millimetre can feel very significant to the tongue and jaw. This heightened sensitivity is normal immediately following crown placement and does not necessarily indicate a problem requiring urgent intervention.

The Dental Science Behind Occlusion and Crown Fit

Understanding how bite forces work can help explain why crown adjustments sometimes require more than one appointment.

Occlusion is the term used to describe how your upper and lower teeth come together when you bite and chew. A well-balanced occlusion distributes biting forces evenly across multiple teeth. When a crown is placed, even a slight change in height can shift this force distribution, causing the crowned tooth to absorb disproportionate pressure.

The periodontal ligament — a thin layer of fibrous tissue surrounding each tooth root — acts as a natural shock absorber. When a crown is too high, the periodontal ligament experiences increased loading with every bite. Over time, this can cause the tooth to feel bruised, sensitive, or sore to pressure.

Additionally, the muscles of the jaw, particularly the masseter and temporalis, are highly sensitive to changes in bite position. If a crown introduces an imbalance, these muscles may compensate by slightly altering jaw movement patterns, which can result in symptoms extending beyond the crowned tooth — including jaw tenderness, headache, or discomfort when chewing.

Understanding this helps explain why patients sometimes describe a crown as feeling high even after being told the bite looks correct on paper.

Common Symptoms of a Crown That Is Too High

Recognising the signs that your crown height may need further review can help you decide when to contact your dental practice. Common indicators include:

  • Sensitivity to pressure — pain or discomfort when biting down directly on the crowned tooth
  • Aching or bruised sensation — a dull ache in or around the tooth that may worsen with eating
  • Jaw muscle tenderness — soreness in the muscles of the jaw, particularly on the side of the crowned tooth
  • Headache — tension headaches, particularly across the temples or at the side of the face
  • Difficulty chewing comfortably — a tendency to avoid chewing on one side
  • Sensitivity to temperature — some patients experience increased hot or cold sensitivity, particularly if the surrounding gum tissue is inflamed

Many of these symptoms settle naturally within one to two weeks as the bite adjusts and any post-procedural sensitivity resolves. However, if symptoms persist or worsen, it is appropriate to contact your dental practice for a review appointment.

How Many Adjustments Might Be Needed?

There is no single answer to how many bite adjustments may be required following crown placement, as this varies depending on the individual, the tooth involved, and the complexity of the case.

For most patients, one adjustment is sufficient. However, it is not unusual to require two or occasionally three small adjustments, particularly in the following situations:

  • Crowns placed on back molar teeth, which experience greater biting forces
  • Cases where the patient had a temporary crown for an extended period
  • Situations where the patient has existing bite imbalances or a history of tooth clenching or grinding (bruxism)
  • Patients who are particularly sensitive to subtle changes in occlusion

Each adjustment involves the dentist marking the contact points with articulating paper and carefully reducing any high spots using a fine-grit polishing bur. The adjustments made are minimal — often removing only microns of material — and do not structurally compromise a well-made crown.

If you have had more than two adjustments and the crown still feels uncomfortable, it is reasonable to request a more thorough occlusal assessment. In some cases, your dentist may refer you to a clinician with advanced training in complex bite issues.

When Should You Contact Your Dentist?

Mild discomfort in the days immediately following crown placement is common and usually settles on its own. However, certain symptoms suggest that a professional review would be appropriate.

You should contact your dental practice if you experience:

  • Persistent pain when biting that has not improved after two weeks
  • Spontaneous toothache — pain that occurs without any biting or temperature stimulus
  • Swelling around the tooth or in the surrounding gum
  • Increased sensitivity to hot or cold that was not present before crown placement
  • A clicking or shifting sensation when biting down
  • Visible changes to the crown, such as chipping or cracking

These symptoms do not necessarily indicate a serious problem, but they do warrant professional assessment. Pain that occurs without any stimulus — particularly at night — may sometimes suggest an issue with the tooth's nerve that requires further investigation.

It is always preferable to contact your dental team early rather than allowing discomfort to persist. Most practices will offer a short review appointment for post-treatment concerns such as bite adjustment.

The Role of Bruxism in Crown Discomfort

Tooth grinding and clenching — clinically known as bruxism — is a significant factor in post-crown discomfort that is frequently overlooked. Many patients are unaware they grind or clench their teeth, as it often occurs during sleep.

If you have a history of bruxism, or if your dentist has observed signs of tooth wear consistent with grinding, your new crown may be more susceptible to feeling high or uncomfortable. This is because bruxism creates exceptionally high bite forces — often far exceeding those generated during normal chewing — which amplify any existing contact imbalance.

For patients with bruxism, wearing a custom-fitted occlusal splint (night guard) after crown placement can help protect the restoration and reduce the strain placed on surrounding structures during sleep. This does not replace the need for accurate bite adjustment, but it can significantly reduce discomfort during the recovery period.

If you suspect that tooth grinding may be contributing to your symptoms, raising this with your dentist during your review appointment will allow them to assess for signs of bruxism and discuss protective options.

Prevention and Oral Health Advice

While not all post-crown discomfort can be prevented, there are practical steps you can take to support a smooth recovery and maintain the long-term health of your crown:

  • Attend all follow-up appointments — if your dentist recommends a review one to two weeks after crown placement, try to attend even if symptoms have improved. Early review prevents small issues from becoming larger ones.
  • Report changes promptly — if your bite changes or discomfort increases, contact your dental practice rather than waiting for your next routine appointment.
  • Be mindful of hard foods — avoid very hard or crunchy foods for the first few days after crown placement to allow time for the cement to fully set and your bite to stabilise.
  • Practise good oral hygiene around the crown margin — flossing gently along the gum line where the crown meets the tooth helps prevent gum inflammation, which can contribute to bite sensitivity.
  • Discuss bruxism — if you grind your teeth, speak with your dentist about a protective night guard to reduce wear and force on new restorations.
  • Maintain regular dental check-ups — routine examinations allow your dentist to monitor your crown and surrounding teeth for early signs of wear or occlusal changes.

Good general oral hygiene and a balanced diet can support gum health, which may play a role in how comfortable dental restorations feel over time. Individual outcomes vary and your dental team can advise on what is most appropriate for your circumstances. Key Points to Remember

  • A dental crown can feel too high even after an adjustment, particularly once local anaesthetic has worn off.
  • Subtle bite discrepancies of even a fraction of a millimetre can feel significant to the tongue and jaw muscles.
  • Mild discomfort in the first one to two weeks after crown placement is common and often resolves naturally.
  • Multiple adjustments are sometimes needed and do not indicate a poorly made crown.
  • Symptoms such as persistent pain, spontaneous aching, or swelling should be assessed by your dental team.
  • Bruxism (tooth grinding) can amplify post-crown discomfort and may benefit from a protective night guard.
  • Attending follow-up appointments and maintaining good oral hygiene support long-term crown comfort and longevity.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should a dental crown feel high after fitting?

It is common for a crown to feel slightly high or unusual for the first few days after placement, particularly once the anaesthetic wears off. Most patients find that minor discomfort resolves within one to two weeks as the bite settles and surrounding tissues recover. If the crown continues to feel noticeably high, or if discomfort persists beyond two weeks, a follow-up appointment for bite adjustment is recommended. Your dental team can assess the contact points and make any necessary refinements.

Why does my crown still feel high after the dentist adjusted it?

Even after adjustment, a crown may continue to feel high for several reasons. If the adjustment was made whilst anaesthetic was still active, you may not have been able to feel subtle bite differences accurately. Muscle memory, jaw posture, and gum tissue sensitivity all influence bite perception. Additionally, it can take a short period of time for the jaw muscles to adapt to the new occlusal contact. If the crown continues to feel uncomfortable after a few days, contact your dental practice for a further review.

Can a high crown cause jaw pain or headaches?

Yes, a crown that is too high can place uneven pressure on the jaw muscles and joints, potentially contributing to jaw tenderness, facial muscle ache, or tension-type headaches. This occurs because the muscles of the jaw work harder to compensate for the altered bite position. These symptoms are not unusual and typically resolve once the crown height is correctly adjusted. If you are experiencing significant jaw discomfort alongside crown-related symptoms, mention this during your dental review appointment.

Is it normal to need more than one bite adjustment after a crown?

Yes, it is common to require more than one adjustment. Crown fitting involves assessing very fine contact points, and multiple refinements are sometimes needed to achieve a comfortable and balanced bite. This is particularly common with molar crowns, which experience greater biting forces, or in patients who have underlying bite imbalances. Each adjustment involves removing only a very small amount of material, and your crown's integrity is not compromised by this process.

Could the discomfort mean there is a problem with my tooth's nerve?

In some cases, discomfort following crown placement may relate to the underlying tooth's nerve rather than crown height alone. This is more likely if you experience spontaneous pain — aching that occurs without biting or temperature change — or significant sensitivity to hot and cold that does not settle after two weeks. These symptoms do not automatically indicate a serious problem, but they do warrant assessment. Your dentist can evaluate the tooth and advise on whether further investigation is appropriate.

What can I do at home if my crown feels too high?

At home, you can try to avoid chewing hard or crunchy foods on the side of the crown, and take over-the-counter pain relief in line with the manufacturer's guidance if discomfort is mild. Rinsing gently with warm saltwater may help soothe any surrounding gum sensitivity. However, home measures are not a substitute for professional adjustment. If your crown feels persistently high or uncomfortable, contact your dental practice to arrange a review. Early assessment is always preferable to waiting when dental discomfort persists.

Conclusion

A dental crown feeling too high — even after a bite adjustment — is a common and understandable concern. The process of achieving a well-balanced bite after crown placement involves multiple factors, from the temporary effects of local anaesthetic to the sensitivity of jaw muscles and the body's natural adaptation period.

For most patients, mild discomfort settles within one to two weeks, and a second or third minor adjustment resolves any remaining bite imbalance. Understanding the dental science behind occlusion helps explain why this can take time and why the experience of bite pressure is so subjective.

That said, persistent or worsening symptoms — particularly spontaneous pain, swelling, or significant sensitivity — should always be assessed professionally. Early review by your dental team is the most reliable way to determine whether further adjustment, or another form of assessment, is needed.

If you have concerns about a crown, we encourage you to contact your dental practice and arrange a review appointment. Ongoing discomfort following restorative treatment is not something you need to simply tolerate.
