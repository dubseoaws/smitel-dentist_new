Introduction

Many people notice, often gradually, that their teeth appear shorter, flatter, or less defined than they once did. You might catch a glimpse in the mirror and wonder whether your teeth have always looked that way, or whether something has changed. Tooth wear is a surprisingly common dental concern among adults in the UK, and it often progresses quietly over many years before it becomes visually or physically noticeable.

If you have been researching why your teeth look shorter or have changed shape, you are certainly not alone. Tooth wear can affect the way you smile, how comfortably you bite and chew, and even your overall dental health over time.

This article explores what causes teeth to change shape through wear, how a dental crown may help restore a worn tooth, and what factors a dental professional would typically consider when assessing your suitability for treatment. Understanding the issue is the first step towards making an informed decision about your dental health.

Can a Dental Crown Restore a Tooth That Has Changed Shape From Wear?

Yes, a dental crown can be an effective option for restoring a tooth that has significantly changed shape due to wear. A crown covers the entire visible surface of a tooth, rebuilding its original height, form, and function. Suitability depends on the extent of wear and overall dental health, which a dentist will assess during a clinical examination.

What Is Tooth Wear and Why Does It Happen?

Tooth wear refers to the gradual loss of tooth structure that occurs over time due to mechanical or chemical forces — rather than from decay or trauma. It is a natural part of ageing to some degree, but in many people it progresses more rapidly than expected.

There are three primary types of tooth wear:

  • Attrition — caused by tooth-to-tooth contact, most commonly associated with bruxism (grinding or clenching the teeth, often during sleep).
  • Erosion — caused by acid dissolving enamel, which can result from dietary habits (frequent consumption of acidic food and drink) or from acid reflux and gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD).
  • Abrasion — caused by external friction, such as overly vigorous brushing or the use of a hard-bristled toothbrush.

In many patients, these types of wear occur in combination, which can accelerate the rate at which teeth lose their shape and height. The back teeth (molars and premolars) are particularly vulnerable because they bear the greatest forces during chewing, though the front teeth are often the first area where people notice visible changes in appearance.

Early identification of the cause of wear is important, because restoring teeth without addressing the underlying cause may reduce the long-term success of any dental treatment.

How Tooth Wear Changes the Shape of Your Teeth

Understanding what is happening to your teeth at a structural level can be helpful when considering your treatment options.

Each tooth is composed of several layers. The outermost layer — enamel — is one of the hardest substances in the human body, but it is not indestructible. Unlike bone, enamel cannot regenerate once it is lost. Beneath enamel lies dentine, a softer, more porous layer that is more vulnerable to both wear and sensitivity.

When enamel is gradually worn away, several changes occur:

  • Teeth appear shorter or flatter, as the biting surfaces lose their natural contour.
  • Edges may become sharper or chipped, particularly on front teeth.
  • Sensitivity can develop, because dentine contains microscopic channels that lead to the nerve of the tooth.
  • The overall bite may shift, which can place additional strain on certain teeth, the jaw joints, and surrounding muscles.

In more advanced cases, the loss of tooth height can affect facial proportions — the lower third of the face may appear to reduce in height, and the lips may fold inward more than before. This is sometimes described by patients as looking older or noticing a change in their facial profile.

What Is a Dental Crown and How Does It Work?

A dental crown is a custom-made restoration that fits over the entire visible portion of a tooth, from the gum line upwards. It is sometimes referred to as a cap. Crowns are fabricated in a dental laboratory to precise measurements taken from impressions or digital scans of your teeth, ensuring a fit that is specific to your mouth.

Dental crowns can be made from several materials, each with different properties:

  • Porcelain or ceramic — highly aesthetic, closely matching natural tooth colour; often used for front teeth.
  • Porcelain-fused-to-metal (PFM) — combines the appearance of porcelain with the strength of a metal substructure.
  • Zirconia — extremely strong and tooth-coloured; suitable for both front and back teeth.
  • Gold or metal alloys — highly durable; typically used for back teeth where appearance is less of a concern.

In the context of tooth wear, a crown serves to rebuild the original shape and height of a worn tooth, protecting the remaining tooth structure and restoring function. Because the crown encases the full tooth, it also provides a degree of protection against further wear to that particular tooth.

The process typically involves two or more appointments: one to prepare the tooth, take impressions or scans, and fit a temporary crown; and a subsequent appointment to fit the permanent restoration.

For patients who have experienced significant wear across multiple teeth, treatment may be more complex and could involve a phased approach or restorations across several teeth to rebuild the bite correctly.

Is a Dental Crown Always the Right Treatment for a Worn Tooth?

While a dental crown for worn teeth is a well-established treatment approach, it is not automatically the right solution for every patient, and suitability must always be determined through a clinical assessment.

For milder degrees of wear, a dentist may first consider less invasive alternatives, such as:

  • Composite bonding — a tooth-coloured resin material applied directly to the tooth to rebuild shape and protect dentine. This involves minimal or no removal of tooth structure.
  • Monitoring and preventative measures — if wear is early-stage and the cause has been identified and managed (for example, through a custom occlusal splint for bruxism), a dentist may recommend active monitoring alongside protective measures rather than immediate restorative treatment.

A dental crown typically becomes the recommended option when:

  • Wear has progressed significantly, and a substantial amount of tooth structure has been lost.
  • The tooth requires additional protection due to its structural fragility.
  • Composite bonding alone would not provide sufficient durability or longevity.
  • The bite (occlusion) needs to be re-established to a correct functional height.

Your dentist will also consider the position of the tooth, the condition of the surrounding gum and bone, and your overall oral health before recommending any course of treatment.

If tooth grinding (bruxism) is identified as a contributing factor, your dentist may recommend a custom-made night guard or occlusal splint for bruxism to protect both your existing teeth and any restorations placed.

When Should You Seek a Professional Dental Assessment?

Many people delay seeking dental advice for tooth wear because the changes occur gradually and are not always associated with pain in the early stages. However, there are several situations in which it would be appropriate to arrange a dental assessment sooner rather than later.

You may wish to book an appointment if you notice:

  • Visible changes in tooth length or shape — particularly if teeth appear noticeably shorter or flatter than before.
  • Increased tooth sensitivity — especially to hot, cold, or sweet foods and drinks, which can suggest that dentine is exposed.
  • Roughness or sharpness along the edges of teeth.
  • Jaw pain, muscle tenderness, or headaches, which can sometimes be associated with bruxism.
  • Difficulty chewing comfortably, or a sense that your bite has shifted.
  • Discolouration, as dentine is naturally more yellow in tone than enamel, so heavily worn teeth may appear darker.

It is worth noting that none of these symptoms are a reliable indicator of any specific diagnosis without a clinical examination. A dentist will be able to assess the degree and pattern of wear, identify potential causes, and discuss suitable management options with you.

Early assessment generally allows for a wider range of treatment options and may help to limit the extent of any restorative work required.

Preventing Further Tooth Wear: What You Can Do

While existing tooth wear cannot be reversed naturally — since enamel does not regenerate — there are practical steps that may help to slow further progression and protect your dental health.

Dietary and lifestyle adjustments:

  • Reduce the frequency of acidic food and drink consumption, including fizzy drinks, citrus fruits, fruit juices, and vinegar-based foods.
  • Rinse your mouth with water after consuming acidic items, but wait at least 30 minutes before brushing, as enamel is temporarily softened by acid and may be more vulnerable to abrasion immediately after eating.
  • Stay well hydrated; a dry mouth can increase the risk of erosion.

Brushing technique:

  • Use a soft-bristled toothbrush and a gentle, circular motion rather than a scrubbing action.
  • Choose a fluoride toothpaste appropriate for your dental needs. Your dentist may recommend a higher-strength fluoride toothpaste in some cases.

Managing bruxism:

  • If you are aware that you grind or clench your teeth — particularly at night — speak to your dentist about a custom-made occlusal splint. This can help to protect teeth from further wear caused by parafunctional habits.

Regular dental check-ups:

  • Attending dental check-ups at the frequency recommended by your dentist allows for early detection and monitoring of wear before it becomes more significant.

Key Points to Remember

  • Tooth wear is the gradual loss of tooth structure caused by grinding, acid erosion, or abrasion — often a combination of these factors.
  • Enamel cannot naturally regenerate once lost, which is why protecting remaining tooth structure is an important priority.
  • A dental crown can effectively restore the shape, height, and function of a tooth that has changed significantly due to wear.
  • Treatment suitability — including whether a crown, composite bonding, or monitoring is appropriate — must be determined through a clinical assessment by a qualified dentist.
  • Addressing the underlying cause of wear (such as bruxism or dietary acid) is important alongside any restorative treatment.
  • Early assessment generally provides more treatment options and may help to minimise the extent of dental work needed.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my teeth have been worn down?

Common signs of tooth wear include teeth appearing shorter or flatter than before, visible changes to the biting edges, increased sensitivity to temperature or sweet foods, and a rougher texture along the edges of teeth. Some people also notice that their smile looks different or that their bite feels less comfortable. These changes can occur gradually over many years, which is why regular dental check-ups are helpful for early detection. A dentist can assess the degree and pattern of wear during a clinical examination.

Is a dental crown painful to have fitted?

The crown fitting process is typically carried out under local anaesthetic, which aims to minimise discomfort during the procedure. Individual experiences may vary. Some patients experience mild sensitivity or gum tenderness after the appointment, particularly around the prepared tooth, but this usually settles within a few days. Your dental team will advise you on what to expect and how to manage any post-procedure discomfort. If you have any concerns about the procedure, discussing them with your dentist before treatment begins is always advisable.

How long does a dental crown last?

The lifespan of a dental crown varies depending on several factors, including the material used, the location of the tooth, your bite, and your ongoing oral hygiene habits. With good care, dental crowns can last for a number of years, though longevity varies between individuals depending on clinical and lifestyle factors. No dental restoration lasts indefinitely, and crowns may eventually require replacement. If tooth wear caused by bruxism was a contributing factor, wearing a protective splint at night can help to extend the life of a crown and other restorations.

Can tooth wear affect my bite or facial appearance?

Yes, in more advanced cases, significant loss of tooth height can affect the way the upper and lower teeth meet (the bite or occlusion). This may place altered forces on certain teeth and the jaw joints. In some individuals, severe wear can also reduce the vertical dimension of the lower face slightly, which may be noticed as a change in facial profile or the appearance of deeper lines around the mouth. Restorative treatment aimed at rebuilding tooth height can help to address these changes, though treatment planning for such cases is typically complex.

Will a crown stop my teeth from wearing further?

A crown protects the specific tooth it covers, but it will not prevent wear from occurring on other teeth if the underlying cause — such as bruxism or dietary acid — has not been addressed. It is important that the cause of wear is identified and managed alongside any restorative treatment. Your dentist may recommend an occlusal splint, dietary adjustments, or other preventative measures to help protect your teeth and any restorations in the long term.

At what stage of tooth wear is a crown usually recommended?

A dental crown is typically considered when tooth wear has progressed to the point where a significant amount of tooth structure has been lost, the tooth is structurally weakened, or less invasive options such as composite bonding are unlikely to provide a sufficiently durable result. For mild or moderate wear, alternative approaches may be more appropriate. The correct stage at which to intervene, and which treatment to choose, can only be determined through a thorough clinical assessment. Conclusion

Tooth wear is a common and often underestimated dental concern that can progressively change the shape, height, and function of your teeth over time. For teeth that have been significantly affected, a dental crown offers a well-established way to restore form, protect remaining tooth structure, and rebuild a comfortable, functional bite.

However, whether a crown is the right solution for your particular situation depends on a range of clinical factors — including the severity and cause of wear, the overall condition of the tooth and surrounding tissues, and your wider dental health. Less invasive approaches may be appropriate in earlier stages, and addressing the underlying cause of wear is always an important part of any treatment plan. If you have noticed changes in the shape or length of your teeth, or if you are experiencing sensitivity or discomfort, arranging a dental assessment is a sensible step. Early advice from a qualified dental professional allows for a broader range of options and may help to limit the extent of treatment needed over time.
