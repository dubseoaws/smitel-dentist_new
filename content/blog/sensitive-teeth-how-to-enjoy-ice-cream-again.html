What Are Sensitive Teeth?

Sensitive teeth â€” clinically referred to as dentine hypersensitivity â€” occur when the underlying layer of your teeth, called dentine, becomes exposed. This exposure allows external stimuli such as cold, hot, sweet, or acidic substances to reach the nerve endings inside the tooth, triggering a short, sharp pain.

Dentine contains thousands of microscopic channels called tubules that lead directly to the tooth's nerve centre, known as the pulp. When the protective outer layers of the tooth â€” enamel on the crown and cementum on the root â€” become worn, damaged, or receded, these tubules are left open. This means that temperature changes and certain foods can stimulate the nerve more easily, resulting in that characteristic sudden discomfort.

Sensitivity can affect a single tooth, several teeth, or all of your teeth. It may come and go over time, or it may become a more persistent concern. While it is a very common condition, it is worth understanding the potential causes, as sensitivity can sometimes indicate an underlying dental issue that may benefit from professional assessment.

Common Causes of Tooth Sensitivity

Understanding what contributes to sensitive teeth can help you take more informed steps towards managing the condition. There are several factors that may lead to or worsen dentine exposure and sensitivity.

Enamel Erosion

Enamel is the hard, protective outer layer of the tooth. Over time, it can be worn away by acidic foods and drinks â€” such as citrus fruits, fizzy drinks, and wine â€” or by conditions such as acid reflux. Once enamel is lost, it does not regenerate, leaving the dentine beneath more vulnerable.

Gum Recession

When the gums recede or pull back from the tooth, the root surface becomes exposed. Unlike the crown of the tooth, roots are not covered by enamel and are therefore more susceptible to sensitivity. Gum recession can occur gradually with age, due to periodontal (gum) disease, or as a result of overly aggressive brushing.

Tooth Grinding (Bruxism)

Habitual grinding or clenching of the teeth â€” often during sleep â€” can gradually wear down enamel and place excessive pressure on teeth, contributing to sensitivity over time.

Cracked or Chipped Teeth

Physical damage to a tooth can expose the dentine or even the pulp, creating pathways for stimuli to reach the nerve.

Overzealous Brushing

Brushing too hard or using a hard-bristled toothbrush can wear away enamel and irritate the gums, both of which may increase sensitivity.

The Dental Science Behind Sensitivity

To understand why sensitive teeth cause discomfort, it helps to know a little about tooth anatomy.

Each tooth is made up of several layers. The outermost visible layer is enamel, which is the hardest substance in the human body. Beneath the enamel lies dentine, a yellowish tissue that makes up the bulk of the tooth structure. At the very centre is the pulp, which contains blood vessels and nerves that keep the tooth alive and responsive.

Dentine is not solid â€” it is filled with thousands of tiny fluid-filled tubules that run from the outer surface of the dentine inwards towards the pulp. When dentine is exposed, changes in temperature or pressure can cause the fluid within these tubules to move. This fluid movement stimulates nerve endings in the pulp, producing the sensation of pain. This mechanism is known as the hydrodynamic theory of dentine sensitivity and is widely accepted in dental science.

This is why cold stimuli â€” such as ice cream â€” tend to provoke the most noticeable response. The rapid temperature change causes the fluid in the tubules to contract quickly, triggering a sharp nerve response. Understanding this process can help explain why certain protective measures, such as using desensitising toothpaste, work by blocking or narrowing these tubules to reduce fluid movement.

How Sensitive Teeth Are Assessed

If you experience tooth sensitivity, a dental professional can carry out a thorough clinical examination to help determine the likely cause. This assessment may include:

  • Visual examination of the teeth and gums for signs of enamel wear, recession, cracks, or decay
  • Sensitivity testing using air or cold stimuli to identify which teeth are affected
  • Dental X-rays to check for underlying issues such as decay, fractures, or bone loss around the teeth
  • Assessment of your bite to look for signs of grinding or clenching

It is important to note that sensitivity can sometimes be a symptom of other dental conditions, such as tooth decay, a cracked tooth, or gum disease. A clinical examination helps to distinguish between simple dentine hypersensitivity and other conditions that may require specific treatment. This is why self-diagnosis based on symptoms alone is not recommended â€” what feels like sensitivity may occasionally indicate a different underlying concern that benefits from early attention.

Practical Tips for Managing Sensitive Teeth

While professional dental advice is always recommended for persistent sensitivity, there are several practical steps that may help manage day-to-day discomfort.

Use a Desensitising Toothpaste

Desensitising toothpastes contain active ingredients â€” such as potassium nitrate or stannous fluoride â€” that can help block the transmission of sensation from the tooth surface to the nerve. These products typically need to be used consistently over several weeks before a noticeable difference is felt.

Switch to a Soft-Bristled Toothbrush

A soft-bristled toothbrush is gentler on enamel and gum tissue. Brushing with a gentle circular motion rather than vigorous back-and-forth scrubbing can help protect against further enamel wear and gum recession.

Be Mindful of Acidic Foods and Drinks

Limiting the frequency of acidic food and drink consumption â€” and avoiding brushing immediately after consuming them â€” can help protect enamel. Waiting at least 30 minutes after eating acidic foods before brushing allows saliva to naturally neutralise acid and begin remineralising the tooth surface.

Avoid Extreme Temperatures

If cold foods trigger discomfort, you might try allowing ice cream to soften slightly before eating, or using a straw for cold drinks to reduce direct contact with sensitive teeth.

Consider a Mouthguard for Grinding

If bruxism is a contributing factor, a custom-made dental mouthguard may help protect your teeth from further wear during sleep.

Treatment Options Your Dentist May Discuss

When sensitivity persists despite home care measures, a range of professional treatment options may be considered following a clinical assessment. The suitability of any treatment depends on the underlying cause and severity of the sensitivity.

Fluoride Varnish or Gel Application

Professional-strength fluoride treatments can be applied to sensitive areas to help strengthen enamel and reduce the transmission of sensations through the dentine tubules.

Bonding or Sealants

In cases where root surfaces are exposed, a dentist may apply a bonding resin or sealant to cover and protect the vulnerable dentine. This can provide a physical barrier against external stimuli.

Gum Grafting

For significant gum recession, a specialist periodontist may recommend gum grafting procedures to cover exposed root surfaces. This is typically considered when recession is advanced and affecting comfort or oral health.

Addressing Underlying Conditions

If sensitivity is caused by decay, a cracked tooth, or gum disease, treating the underlying condition is essential. Your dentist will discuss the most appropriate options based on your individual clinical findings.

It is important to understand that treatment outcomes vary between patients, and the most suitable approach can only be determined through a thorough clinical examination.

When Professional Dental Assessment May Be Needed

While occasional mild sensitivity is common and may respond well to home care, there are certain situations where seeking a professional dental assessment is advisable.

You may wish to consider booking a dental appointment if you experience:

  • Sensitivity that persists for more than a few weeks despite using desensitising toothpaste
  • Pain that lingers after the stimulus (such as cold or heat) has been removed
  • Sensitivity localised to one specific tooth, which may suggest decay, a crack, or other structural issue
  • Spontaneous toothache that occurs without an obvious trigger
  • Swelling, redness, or tenderness in the gums around the affected area
  • Sensitivity following a dental procedure, which is common but should be monitored if it does not settle

These symptoms do not necessarily indicate a serious problem, but they may benefit from professional evaluation to rule out conditions that could worsen if left unaddressed. Early assessment often leads to simpler and more comfortable management.

If you are experiencing any of these symptoms, a general dental check-up can provide clarity and peace of mind.

  • Maintain a consistent oral hygiene routine â€” brush twice daily with a fluoride toothpaste and clean between your teeth daily using interdental brushes or floss
  • Use a soft-bristled toothbrush and replace it every three to four months, or sooner if the bristles become frayed
  • Limit acidic food and drink consumption â€” when you do consume acidic items, try to have them with meals rather than on their own, and rinse with water afterwards
  • Stay hydrated â€” adequate water intake supports saliva production, which is your mouth's natural defence against acid and bacteria
  • Attend regular dental check-ups â€” routine examinations allow your dentist to identify early signs of enamel wear, gum recession, or other issues before they progress
  • Discuss grinding habits with your dentist â€” if you suspect you grind your teeth, professional advice on protective measures can help prevent further damage
  • Avoid using your teeth as tools â€” opening packaging or biting hard objects can cause chips and cracks that contribute to sensitivity

Consistent preventative care is one of the most effective ways to maintain comfortable, healthy teeth for the long term.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why are my teeth sensitive to cold but not hot?

Cold sensitivity is often associated with exposed dentine, where the rapid temperature drop causes fluid movement within the microscopic tubules of the tooth, stimulating nerve endings. Hot sensitivity, while also possible, can sometimes indicate a different type of dental issue, such as inflammation of the pulp (pulpitis). If you notice sensitivity primarily to cold, it may suggest early-stage enamel wear or gum recession. However, if you develop sensitivity to heat â€” particularly if the pain lingers â€” it is advisable to seek a dental assessment, as this may indicate a condition that benefits from prompt attention.

Can sensitive teeth heal on their own?

In some cases, mild sensitivity may improve over time, particularly if it is caused by temporary factors such as recent dental treatment or short-term dietary habits. Using desensitising toothpaste consistently can also help reduce symptoms gradually. However, sensitivity caused by enamel loss, significant gum recession, or tooth damage is unlikely to resolve without some form of intervention. Since enamel does not regenerate once lost, early professional advice can help identify the cause and prevent further progression. Self-management is helpful, but it works best alongside professional guidance.

Is tooth sensitivity a sign of a cavity?

Tooth sensitivity can sometimes be an early indicator of dental decay, particularly if it is localised to a specific tooth. However, sensitivity has many possible causes, including enamel erosion, gum recession, and bruxism, so it does not automatically mean a cavity is present. The only reliable way to determine whether decay is involved is through a clinical dental examination, which may include X-rays. If your sensitivity is new, persistent, or worsening, a dental check-up can help clarify the cause and provide appropriate guidance.

What toothpaste is best for sensitive teeth?

Toothpastes designed for sensitive teeth typically contain active ingredients such as potassium nitrate, which helps calm the nerve inside the tooth, or stannous fluoride, which can help block exposed dentine tubules. Look for products that carry the British Dental Association (BDA) accreditation or similar endorsements. It is important to use desensitising toothpaste regularly â€” ideally twice daily â€” as the protective effects build up over time with consistent use. Your dentist can recommend a specific product based on your individual needs and the nature of your sensitivity.

Can teeth whitening cause sensitivity?

Tooth sensitivity is a commonly reported temporary side effect of teeth whitening procedures. The bleaching agents used in whitening treatments can temporarily increase the permeability of enamel, allowing stimuli to reach the nerve more easily. This sensitivity is usually short-lived and tends to resolve within a few days to a couple of weeks after treatment. If you have pre-existing sensitivity, it is important to discuss this with your dentist before undergoing any whitening treatment, as they can advise on whether the procedure is suitable and suggest ways to minimise discomfort.

How often should I visit the dentist if I have sensitive teeth?

If you experience ongoing tooth sensitivity, your dentist may recommend more frequent check-ups to monitor the condition and assess whether any underlying cause is progressing. For most patients, a dental examination every six to twelve months is generally recommended, though your dentist will advise on the most appropriate interval based on your individual oral health. Regular visits allow early detection of enamel wear, gum recession, or decay, enabling timely intervention that may prevent sensitivity from worsening.
