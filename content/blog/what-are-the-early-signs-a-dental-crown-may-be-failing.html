Introduction

If you have a dental crown and have started to notice something feels different — perhaps a subtle sensitivity, an unusual sensation when biting, or a slight change in how the crown sits — you are not alone in wondering whether something might be wrong. Many patients search online for reassurance or guidance when they suspect their crown may not be performing as it should.

Dental crowns are long-lasting restorations, but like all dental work, they are not permanent. Over time, various factors can affect their integrity. Understanding the early signs that a failing dental crown may be developing can help you act promptly and avoid more complex problems further down the line.

This article explains what a dental crown is, why crowns can sometimes fail, the early warning signs to be aware of, and when it may be appropriate to seek professional dental advice. All information here is educational, and individual concerns should always be assessed by a qualified dental professional.

What Is a Dental Crown and How Long Should It Last?

A dental crown is a tooth-shaped cap placed over a damaged, weakened, or heavily restored tooth. It is designed to restore the tooth's shape, size, strength, and appearance. Crowns are commonly used following root canal treatment, to protect a cracked tooth, or to restore a tooth that has broken down significantly.

Crowns can be made from a variety of materials, including porcelain, ceramic, metal alloys, or a combination of porcelain fused to metal. Each material has different properties affecting durability, appearance, and suitability for different locations in the mouth.

With proper care, a dental crown may last for many years, commonly around 10 to 15 years. However, lifespan varies and depends on factors including oral hygiene habits, bite forces, the location of the crown in the mouth, and the health of the underlying tooth and surrounding gum tissue.

It is worth noting that no dental restoration lasts indefinitely. Regular dental check-ups allow your dentist to monitor the condition of existing crowns and identify any early signs of wear or deterioration before they develop into larger problems.

What Are the Early Signs a Dental Crown May Be Failing?

Early signs of a failing dental crown include increased sensitivity to hot, cold, or sweet foods, discomfort or pain when biting, a visible crack or chip in the crown, a loose or shifting sensation, gum recession around the crown margin, or a persistent bad taste. Any of these symptoms warrants a professional dental assessment.

Common Reasons Why Dental Crowns Can Fail

Understanding why crowns fail helps put the warning signs into context. There is rarely a single cause — most crown failures develop gradually due to a combination of factors.

Natural wear over time is one of the most common contributors. Even high-quality materials experience wear through the repeated forces of chewing and grinding. Over many years, the outer surface of a crown can thin, chip, or fracture.

Tooth decay beneath the crown is another significant cause. Although the crown itself cannot decay, the natural tooth structure underneath remains susceptible to caries, particularly at the margin where the crown meets the tooth and gumline. If bacteria infiltrate this junction, decay can develop undetected beneath the restoration.

Bruxism (teeth grinding or clenching) places excessive force on crowns, particularly at night. This can accelerate wear, cause fractures, and loosen the crown over time.

Cement failure can also occur. Crowns are bonded into place using dental cement, and over time this bond can weaken, especially if saliva contamination occurred during placement or if the crown experiences repeated lateral forces.

Finally, gum recession or gum disease can expose the crown margin, creating a gap where bacteria can accumulate and decay can develop.

Early Warning Signs That a Dental Crown May Be Failing

Recognising the early signs of a failing dental crown early can make a meaningful difference to the outcome and the complexity of any treatment required. The following are the most commonly reported signs patients experience.

1. Increased Tooth Sensitivity

If you begin to notice sensitivity to hot, cold, or sweet foods and drinks around a crowned tooth — especially if this is a new sensation — it may indicate that the crown's seal has weakened or that decay has begun to develop at the margin. Mild sensitivity can have other explanations, but persistent or worsening sensitivity should be assessed.

2. Pain or Discomfort When Biting

A well-fitted crown should allow comfortable, even biting pressure. If you notice pain, discomfort, or a sharp sensation when you bite or chew, this could suggest the crown has shifted, cracked, or that the underlying tooth is under stress. Bite-related pain that persists beyond a few days deserves professional evaluation.

3. Visible Cracks or Chips

Porcelain and ceramic crowns, while natural-looking, can chip or crack under heavy forces. If you can see or feel a rough edge, a visible fracture, or a fragment appears to be missing, the crown's structural integrity may be compromised. Even small chips can create rough surfaces that harbour bacteria.

4. A Loose or Shifting Sensation

If the crown feels loose, rocks slightly, or shifts when you press on it, the dental cement binding it in place may have weakened or failed. A loose crown can allow bacteria to enter beneath the restoration, increasing the risk of decay and infection.

5. Gum Changes Around the Crown

Healthy gums should fit snugly around the base of the crown. If you notice gum recession, swelling, redness, or tenderness around a crowned tooth, this may indicate gum disease has developed or that the crown margin is no longer sealing effectively. These are important signs to discuss with your dentist.

6. A Persistent Bad Taste or Odour

A persistent bad taste or unpleasant odour emanating from around a crowned tooth may indicate bacterial activity beneath or around the crown, possibly associated with decay or early infection. This symptom should not be ignored.

The Dental Science Behind Crown Failure

To understand how and why crowns fail, it helps to consider the anatomy of the tooth and the nature of dental restorations.

A crowned tooth still has a living root (unless it has had root canal treatment) and a layer of dentine beneath the crown. Dentine contains microscopic tubules that connect to the nerve of the tooth. When the crown's seal deteriorates — whether through cement failure, marginal breakdown, or cracking — bacteria and temperature changes can reach these tubules, causing sensitivity or pain.

Tooth decay (dental caries) is caused by acid-producing bacteria that break down tooth structure. Even though the crown cap itself is impervious to decay, the junction between the crown and the natural tooth — known as the crown margin — can accumulate plaque if not cleaned effectively. Over time, acid from bacterial biofilm can dissolve the tooth structure at this margin, creating what dentists refer to as secondary or recurrent decay.

Additionally, the periodontal ligament — the soft tissue that anchors the tooth root to the jawbone — can be affected by gum disease progression around a crowned tooth, further destabilising the restoration. This is why supporting gum health is as important as caring for the crown itself.

When a Professional Dental Assessment May Be Appropriate

Whilst not every new sensation around a crowned tooth indicates an emergency, there are several situations where it is sensible to arrange a dental appointment rather than wait and see.

Consider contacting your dentist if you experience:

  • Persistent sensitivity that does not resolve within a few days
  • Pain when biting or chewing that continues beyond 48–72 hours
  • A visible crack, chip, or fracture in the crown
  • A loose crown or one that feels unstable
  • Swelling, tenderness, or changes in the gum tissue around a crowned tooth
  • A persistent unpleasant taste or odour around the tooth
  • Any crown that has come off completely

A crown that has become loose or detached should be reviewed promptly, as the exposed tooth structure can be vulnerable to damage and sensitivity. If you cannot see your dentist immediately, keep the crown safe if it has come out, avoid chewing on that side of the mouth, and contact the practice to arrange an urgent appointment.

If you are concerned about any changes with an existing crown, arranging urgent dental assessment can help identify the cause promptly and protect the underlying tooth.

Treatment Options When a Crown Shows Signs of Failing

Treatment will always depend on the nature and extent of the problem, and can only be determined following a clinical examination. General approaches a dentist may consider include the following.

Re-cementing a loose crown may be appropriate if the crown itself is intact, the underlying tooth is healthy, and the crown simply requires re-bonding. This is often a straightforward procedure.

Crown replacement may be recommended if the crown is cracked, chipped beyond repair, does not fit correctly, or is very old. A new crown would be fabricated to fit the current state of the tooth.

Treatment of underlying decay will be needed before any new crown can be placed if secondary decay has developed beneath the existing restoration. The extent of the decay will determine how much tooth structure can be preserved.

Root canal treatment may sometimes be necessary if decay or infection has reached the pulp of the tooth. Following root canal therapy, a new crown is typically placed to protect the treated tooth.

For patients who need this pathway, your dentist may discuss root canal treatment before final restorative planning.

Periodontal treatment may be required if gum disease has developed around the crowned tooth, to stabilise the gum and bone before any further restorative work is considered.

Prevention and Caring for Your Dental Crown

Whilst no crown lasts forever, there are practical steps you can take to help protect your restoration and extend its lifespan.

Maintain good oral hygiene. Brush twice daily with fluoride toothpaste and clean between teeth daily using floss or interdental brushes. Pay particular attention to the gumline around crowned teeth, as plaque accumulation at the crown margin is a key risk factor for secondary decay.

Attend regular dental check-ups. Routine examinations allow your dentist to monitor the condition of your crowns, check for early signs of wear or marginal breakdown, and take preventative action before problems escalate. Most dental professionals recommend check-ups every six to twelve months, depending on individual risk factors.

Wear a night guard if recommended. If you grind or clench your teeth, a custom-made occlusal splint (night guard) can help protect crowns and other restorations from the excessive forces that bruxism generates.

Avoid habits that put crowns at risk. Chewing on ice, biting hard objects such as pens or fingernails, or using your teeth to open packaging can chip or crack porcelain crowns. These habits are best avoided.

Be mindful of very sticky or hard foods. Whilst a well-made crown should handle normal chewing forces, very hard or sticky foods can place additional stress on the restoration and the underlying cement.

Good overall oral health, including managing gum disease risk, supports the longevity of dental restorations. If you are interested in maintaining your oral health between appointments, a routine dental examination helps detect early changes before they become more complex.

Key Points to Remember

  • Dental crowns are long-lasting but not permanent — they require regular monitoring and maintenance.
  • Early warning signs of a failing dental crown include sensitivity, pain when biting, visible cracks, a loose sensation, gum changes, and persistent bad taste.
  • Secondary decay beneath the crown margin is one of the most common reasons crowns fail over time, underlining the importance of thorough daily cleaning.
  • Bruxism (teeth grinding) can accelerate crown wear and increase the risk of fracture — a night guard may help if this applies to you.
  • Prompt professional assessment is recommended if you notice any changes around a crowned tooth, as early intervention often leads to simpler treatment.
  • Regular dental check-ups are one of the most effective ways to detect crown deterioration before it becomes a more significant problem.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my dental crown is failing?

Common signs include new or worsening sensitivity around the crowned tooth, discomfort or pain when biting, a visible crack or chip in the crown material, a loose or shifting sensation, changes to the gum tissue around the crown, or a persistent bad taste. Not all of these symptoms automatically confirm failure, but any that persist or worsen should be assessed by a dentist. Only a clinical examination, which may include X-rays, can confirm whether a crown is compromised and what action, if any, is needed.

Can a failing dental crown cause infection?

If a dental crown fails and bacteria penetrate beneath the restoration — whether through a loose margin, crack, or secondary decay — infection can develop in the underlying tooth or surrounding tissues. Signs of infection may include spontaneous pain, swelling, sensitivity to pressure, or a bad taste. If you suspect an infection, it is important to contact your dental practice promptly. Infections associated with teeth do not resolve without professional treatment, and early assessment is always preferable.

How long does a dental crown typically last?

With good oral hygiene and regular dental check-ups, a well-placed crown may last many years, often around 10 to 15 years, though this can vary. Some patients may keep crowns for substantially longer, while others may need replacement sooner due to factors such as bruxism, decay, or gum recession. The longevity of a crown depends on the material used, its location in the mouth, how well it is cleaned, and individual factors such as bite habits. Your dentist can advise on the expected lifespan of your specific restoration.

Is it normal to have sensitivity after a new crown is fitted?

Mild sensitivity in the days immediately following a new crown being fitted is relatively common and often settles as the tooth adjusts to the restoration. Sensitivity to temperature or pressure during the initial healing period is generally not a cause for concern. However, if sensitivity is severe, worsening, or persists beyond two to three weeks, it is worth discussing with your dentist. This may indicate that the crown requires bite adjustment or that the underlying tooth requires further assessment.

Can a loose dental crown be re-cemented?

In some cases, yes. If a crown has become loose but remains intact and the underlying tooth is healthy and free from decay, a dentist may be able to clean the crown and the tooth surface and rebond it with fresh dental cement. However, this is not always possible — if the underlying tooth has decayed significantly, or if the crown is cracked or no longer fits correctly, replacement may be more appropriate. The suitability of re-cementing can only be assessed during a clinical examination.

How should I care for my teeth if my dental crown has come off?

If your crown comes off completely, try to keep it safe and contact your dental practice as soon as possible to arrange an assessment. In the meantime, avoid chewing on that side of the mouth and be mindful that the exposed tooth may be sensitive. Over-the-counter dental cement (available from pharmacies) can be used as a very temporary measure to protect the tooth if you are unable to see a dentist quickly, but this is not a substitute for professional treatment. Do not attempt to glue a crown back permanently yourself.

Conclusion

Dental crowns play an important role in protecting and restoring damaged teeth, and with appropriate care, they can serve patients well for many years. However, like all dental restorations, they are not indestructible and can show signs of wear or failure over time. Recognising the early warning signs of a failing dental crown — such as sensitivity, pain when biting, visible damage, looseness, or gum changes — and acting on them promptly can make a significant difference to the ease and success of any subsequent treatment.

Good daily oral hygiene, regular attendance for dental check-ups, and being mindful of habits that place unnecessary stress on restorations all contribute to supporting their longevity. If you have any concerns about a crown or notice any changes in how it feels or looks, the most constructive step is always to discuss them with a qualified dental professional.

Dental symptoms and treatment options should always be assessed individually during a clinical examination.
