Introduction

It is a surprisingly common concern amongst patients who have recently had dental implant treatment: everything looks visually acceptable in the mirror, yet the implant feels noticeably high when biting down. If you have found yourself pressing your teeth together and wondering whether something is slightly off, you are certainly not alone in searching for answers online.

A dental implant that feels too high — even when it appears visually normal — can be a genuinely confusing experience. The discomfort can range from a subtle awareness of pressure to a more persistent ache when chewing. Understanding why this happens, what it means from a clinical perspective, and when it may be worth mentioning to your dentist can help put your mind at ease.

This article explains the possible reasons a dental implant crown may feel high, how bite occlusion works, what symptoms to look out for, and when a professional assessment may be beneficial. As with all dental concerns, a clinical examination is always the most reliable way to understand what is happening in your individual situation.

Can a Dental Implant Feel Too High Even If It Looks Fine?

Yes. A dental implant can feel too high due to an occlusal discrepancy — meaning the implant crown sits slightly above the correct bite height. Even a difference of fractions of a millimetre can be detected by the jaw. This is a relatively common and usually correctable issue that a dentist can assess and adjust during a clinical review appointment.

Understanding Dental Implant Occlusion: Why Bite Height Matters

One of the most important — yet least discussed — aspects of dental implant treatment is occlusion. Occlusion simply refers to the way your upper and lower teeth meet when you bite or chew. In natural dentition, the bite is calibrated over years of wear, movement, and jaw adaptation. When a dental implant crown is placed, it must be carefully shaped and positioned to integrate seamlessly into this existing bite pattern.

Even a difference of a fraction of a millimetre in crown height can be detected by the periodontal ligament surrounding natural teeth, or by the sensory receptors in the jaw muscles and joints. Natural teeth have a degree of micro-movement due to their periodontal ligament. Dental implants, however, are osseointegrated — meaning they are fused directly into the bone without this shock-absorbing ligament. This difference in mobility means that even very minor bite discrepancies can feel more pronounced on an implant than they might on a natural tooth.

This phenomenon explains why some patients report that their implant feels high very soon after placement, even when the crown appears visually normal. The absence of a periodontal ligament means the implant has no natural give, making the bite sensation particularly sensitive to any inaccuracy in crown height.

Common Reasons a Dental Implant Crown May Feel Too High

There are several reasons why a dental implant crown might feel elevated above the correct bite level. Understanding these can help clarify what may have happened in your case, though a clinical examination is always necessary to confirm the cause.

Occlusal Adjustment Needed at Fitting When a crown is fabricated in a dental laboratory, it is made to a precise specification. However, small discrepancies can occur between the model used in the laboratory and the actual position of your teeth. Once the crown is placed, minor adjustments to its biting surface are common and expected as part of normal finishing. If these adjustments were insufficient, the crown may sit fractionally too high.

Temporary Anaesthesia Masking Sensation If the crown was placed shortly after an injection of local anaesthetic, you may not have been able to accurately assess the bite height during fitting. Once the numbness wears off, the true occlusal relationship becomes apparent, and patients may notice the crown feels elevated.

Jaw Swelling or Tissue Changes Post-Placement In the days following implant placement or crown fitting, localised swelling and tissue changes can temporarily affect how the bite feels. As healing progresses, the sensation may shift.

Bone or Tissue Settlement Over time, slight changes in the surrounding bone or soft tissue can affect the position of the implant crown relative to neighbouring teeth.

How the Jaw Detects Bite Discrepancies: The Clinical Science

To appreciate why a dental implant can feel too high even when it looks visually correct, it helps to understand a little about how the jaw perceives bite force.

Natural teeth are embedded in the jaw through a structure called the periodontal ligament (PDL). This network of fibres not only holds the tooth in its socket but is packed with proprioceptive nerve endings — sensory receptors that detect pressure, direction, and force. These receptors communicate information to the brain about how hard and at what angle the teeth are meeting.

Dental implants bypass this system entirely. Because the implant fuses directly with the jawbone (a process called osseointegration), there is no periodontal ligament and therefore no PDL-based proprioception. Instead, bite sensation is mediated through receptors in the surrounding bone, gum tissue, and jaw musculature — systems that are sensitive but respond differently to force than the PDL does.

This difference means that even tiny variations in crown height — sometimes as little as 0.1mm — can be felt more keenly on an implant than on a natural tooth. What might go unnoticed on a natural tooth can feel distinctly uncomfortable on an implant. This is not a flaw in the implant system; it is simply a reflection of how different the sensory landscape is around an osseointegrated implant compared to a natural tooth with its ligament intact.

Understanding this science helps explain why bite adjustment after implant crown placement is a normal and expected part of the treatment process, not an indication that something has gone seriously wrong.

Symptoms That May Suggest Your Implant Bite Needs Reviewing

Most patients with a slightly high implant crown experience relatively mild symptoms, particularly in the early stages. Being aware of what to look out for can help you decide whether to contact your dental practice for a review appointment.

Common signs that the implant bite may need adjustment include:

  • A feeling of the implant crown hitting first when the teeth come together
  • Discomfort or sensitivity when biting or chewing on that side
  • A dull ache or soreness in the jaw that worsens throughout the day
  • Tension or fatigue in the jaw muscles, particularly on the affected side
  • Headaches, particularly in the temples or along the jaw, that appear following implant placement
  • Neighbouring natural teeth feeling sore or under pressure

It is worth noting that some initial awareness of the implant after placement is normal and expected. The mouth is highly sensitive to change, and the brain takes time to adjust to new dental restorations. However, if the sensation does not reduce within a week or two, or if discomfort is increasing rather than settling, this may indicate that a bite adjustment would be beneficial.

If you are experiencing any of these symptoms following dental implant treatment, it is worth exploring dental implant aftercare guidance to better understand what the normal recovery process involves and when to seek further advice.

What Happens If a High Implant Bite Is Left Unaddressed?

Understanding the potential consequences of an unaddressed occlusal discrepancy is important, though it is equally important not to cause unnecessary concern. Many patients have minor bite adjustments made without any long-term complications, and early correction tends to resolve the issue simply and effectively.

However, if a bite discrepancy is persistent and not assessed, there are some considerations worth being aware of:

Stress on the Implant Components Because dental implants are rigid and fused to the bone, repeated excessive biting force on a high crown can place mechanical stress on the implant fixture, the abutment, or the crown itself. Over time, this may contribute to component loosening or, in some cases, complications with osseointegration.

Temporomandibular Joint (TMJ) Discomfort An uneven bite can affect the jaw joint and surrounding muscles. Patients may experience jaw clicking, stiffness, or discomfort in the TMJ area if the bite is consistently unbalanced over a prolonged period.

Adjacent Tooth Sensitivity The natural teeth adjacent to the implant may experience increased loading if bite forces are being redistributed, which could contribute to sensitivity or wear over time.

It is important to emphasise that these are potential considerations in cases where a bite issue persists unaddressed — they are not inevitable outcomes. Most cases are resolved straightforwardly with a minor occlusal adjustment at a follow-up appointment.

When Professional Dental Assessment May Be Appropriate

A dental review is advisable if the feeling of a high implant bite is persistent, increasing, or accompanied by additional symptoms. There is no cause for alarm in seeking a review — it is simply good practice following any dental treatment to communicate ongoing concerns to your dental team.

Consider contacting your dental practice if you experience:

  • Persistent bite discomfort lasting more than two weeks after crown placement
  • Pain when biting or chewing that is not gradually improving
  • Swelling or tenderness around the implant site
  • Visible changes to the gum tissue surrounding the implant
  • Unexplained headaches or jaw tension following implant treatment
  • Any sudden change in how the implant feels after a period of comfortable function

A dentist can assess the bite using articulating paper — a thin, pressure-sensitive material that marks the exact points of contact between the teeth during biting. This allows precise identification of any high spots on the crown, which can then be gently adjusted to restore a balanced occlusion. The adjustment process is typically quick, minimally invasive, and does not require anaesthesia.

If you have concerns about how your implant is feeling, booking a dental check-up with your treating dentist or a dental professional experienced in implant care is a straightforward and sensible step.

Prevention and Aftercare: Protecting Your Dental Implant

Whilst a high bite is not always preventable — given the natural variables involved in crown fabrication and fitting — there are practical steps patients can take to support the long-term health of their implant and minimise the risk of bite-related problems developing.

Communicate clearly during fitting appointments If a crown is being fitted under local anaesthetic, inform your dentist that you may not be able to accurately assess the bite until the numbness has worn off. Arrange to return for a bite check once sensation has fully returned.

Attend all recommended follow-up appointments Post-implant review appointments exist precisely to identify and address any issues such as occlusal discrepancies, soft tissue healing concerns, or early signs of peri-implantitis. Attending these reviews is an important part of supporting the long-term health and stability of your implant treatment.

Maintain diligent oral hygiene around the implant Whilst oral hygiene does not directly prevent a high bite, keeping the gum tissue around the implant healthy reduces the risk of complications that could affect the overall stability and comfort of the restoration.

Avoid very hard foods in the early post-placement period Immediately following crown placement, avoiding particularly hard or crunchy foods allows the surrounding tissues to settle and reduces the risk of placing excessive force on a restoration that may still require fine adjustments.

Use a nightguard if you grind your teeth Bruxism (tooth grinding) places significant stress on dental restorations including implant crowns. If you are known to grind your teeth, discussing a custom nightguard with your dentist can help protect the implant from excessive occlusal force.

For comprehensive guidance on caring for your mouth following implant treatment, your dental practice is the most reliable source of personalised advice.

Key Points to Remember

  • A dental implant can feel too high even when it looks visually fine, due to the absence of a periodontal ligament and the jaw's sensitivity to even tiny bite discrepancies.
  • Common causes include minor inaccuracies in crown fabrication, fitting under local anaesthesia, or post-placement tissue changes.
  • Symptoms such as persistent bite discomfort, jaw tension, or localised aching may suggest a bite adjustment is needed.
  • Minor occlusal adjustments are a normal and expected part of the implant process — they do not indicate a treatment failure.
  • If bite discomfort persists beyond two weeks or is accompanied by other symptoms, a dental review appointment is advisable.
  • Maintaining good oral hygiene and attending follow-up appointments helps protect the long-term health of a dental implant.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it normal for a new dental implant crown to feel high at first?

Some initial awareness or sensitivity around a newly placed implant crown is common. The mouth is highly attuned to changes, and it can take a short period for the brain to adjust to the new restoration. However, if the implant consistently feels elevated when biting — particularly if this is accompanied by discomfort — it is worth having the bite assessed by your dentist. Minor adjustments to the crown's occlusal surface are routine and straightforward to perform.

How does a dentist check if a dental implant bite is too high?

Dentists typically use a material called articulating paper to assess bite height. This thin paper is placed between the teeth, and the patient is asked to bite down. The pressure marks left on the crown and surrounding teeth reveal which surfaces are making contact and whether any single point is taking disproportionate force. Based on this information, the dentist can make precise, targeted adjustments to the crown surface to achieve a balanced bite.

Can a high implant bite cause damage over time?

If a significant bite discrepancy remains unaddressed over an extended period, it can place mechanical stress on the implant components and surrounding bone. It may also contribute to discomfort in the jaw joint or muscles. However, most bite discrepancies are identified and resolved at routine follow-up appointments without causing lasting issues. Seeking an early assessment if you are concerned is always the most straightforward approach.

Will bite adjustment affect the appearance of my implant crown?

In most cases, a minor occlusal adjustment involves removing a very small amount of material from the biting surface of the crown — an area that is generally not visible during normal smiling or speaking. The aesthetic appearance of the crown is typically unaffected by bite adjustments. Your dentist will discuss what the adjustment involves before proceeding.

How long after implant placement should I wait before reporting a high bite?

If you feel that the bite is clearly uncomfortable or noticeably uneven immediately after placement, there is no need to wait — you can contact your dental practice at any point. It is reasonable to allow a few days for initial post-treatment sensitivity to settle before making a definitive judgement on bite comfort. However, if the sensation is not improving after one to two weeks, arranging a review appointment is advisable. There is no benefit to delaying assessment if the discomfort is persistent.

Can dental implant bite problems be related to peri-implantitis?

Peri-implantitis is an inflammatory condition affecting the gum and bone tissue surrounding a dental implant, typically associated with bacterial infection rather than bite height. However, because excessive occlusal force may contribute to stress on the bone-implant interface, there can be indirect associations between unresolved bite issues and implant health over time. If you are experiencing symptoms such as swelling, bleeding around the implant site, or looseness of the crown, these warrant prompt professional assessment as they may suggest a different underlying concern. If you would like to understand more about implant health and gum care, speaking with a dental professional is the most reliable approach.

Conclusion

A dental implant that feels too high — even when it looks visually normal — is a concern that many patients experience, and it is one that is generally well understood within implant dentistry. The absence of a periodontal ligament around the implant makes the bite sensation particularly sensitive to even very small variations in crown height. Fortunately, this is typically a straightforward issue to identify and address through a minor occlusal adjustment at a follow-up appointment.

The key message is this: if your implant crown feels elevated, uncomfortable, or inconsistent when biting, it is worth raising this with your dental practice. Early assessment leads to straightforward solutions, and leaving bite discomfort unaddressed for extended periods is unnecessary when help is readily available.
