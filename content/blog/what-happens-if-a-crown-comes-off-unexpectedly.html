Introduction

Discovering that your dental crown has come off can be an unsettling experience, particularly if it happens without warning. Whether it occurs whilst eating, brushing your teeth, or even during sleep, a dislodged crown is one of the more common dental concerns that leads people to search for guidance online. Understandably, questions such as "what should I do now?" or "is this a dental emergency?" can feel urgent in the moment.

A dental crown — a tooth-shaped cap placed over a damaged or restored tooth — is designed to be long-lasting, but it is not permanent. Over time, various factors can cause a crown to loosen or detach entirely. Understanding why this happens and what steps to take can help you respond calmly and appropriately.

This article explains what a dislodged dental crown is, the common reasons it may occur, what you may experience, and when it is appropriate to seek professional dental advice. It is intended to provide educational clarity rather than replace a clinical assessment.

What Happens If a Dental Crown Comes Off?

If a dental crown comes off, keep it safe and avoid placing it back in your mouth without professional guidance. The exposed tooth may feel sensitive or uncomfortable. Contact your dental practice as soon as possible for an assessment. A dentist can evaluate the underlying tooth and determine whether the crown can be re-cemented or whether further treatment is needed.

What Is a Dental Crown and Why Might It Come Off?

A dental crown is a custom-made restoration designed to cover and protect a tooth that has been weakened by decay, damage, or a previous large filling. Crowns can be made from a variety of materials, including porcelain, ceramic, metal alloy, or a combination of these. Once fitted and cemented in place, a well-maintained crown can last many years.

However, a crown coming loose or detaching entirely is not uncommon. Several factors can contribute to this happening:

  • Deterioration of dental cement: The adhesive used to secure a crown can weaken over time due to natural wear, moisture exposure, or age.
  • Tooth decay beneath the crown: If decay develops in the underlying tooth structure, it can compromise the fit and stability of the crown.
  • Physical force or trauma: Biting down on hard foods, grinding teeth (bruxism), or an accidental knock can dislodge a crown.
  • A poorly fitting crown: If the original fit was not ideal, or the tooth has changed shape over time, the crown may become loose.
  • Old restorations: Crowns that have been in place for a decade or more are more susceptible to failure.

Understanding the underlying cause is important, as it helps determine what treatment may be appropriate going forward.

The Dental Science Behind a Crown: Why the Underlying Tooth Matters

To understand why a dislodged crown can be clinically significant, it helps to consider the anatomy involved. The tooth beneath a crown — often referred to as the prepared tooth or abutment — has typically been reshaped and reduced in size to allow the crown to fit over it precisely. This means the underlying tooth has less of its natural enamel and structure than an unrestored tooth.

Natural tooth enamel is the hardest substance in the human body and serves as a protective barrier against bacteria, temperature changes, and physical stress. When a crown is in place, it fulfils this protective function. Once the crown is removed, the prepared tooth is exposed and significantly more vulnerable.

Dentine — the layer beneath enamel — contains microscopic tubules connected to the nerve of the tooth. When exposed, these tubules can transmit sensations of heat, cold, and pressure far more readily, which explains why a tooth with a missing crown often feels heightened sensitivity. If the underlying tooth has also developed decay, this introduces a further risk of bacterial progression toward the pulp — the innermost part of the tooth containing nerves and blood vessels.

This is why prompt assessment by a dental professional matters following a dislodged crown.

What You Might Experience When a Crown Comes Off

The experience of losing a crown can vary considerably from person to person, depending on the condition of the underlying tooth and how long the crown has been in place.

Common sensations and observations may include:

  • Sensitivity to temperature: The exposed tooth may react to hot or cold food and drink more noticeably than usual.
  • Sensitivity to air: Some patients notice a sharp or uncomfortable feeling when cold air reaches the exposed tooth.
  • Pressure sensitivity: Biting or chewing on the affected side may feel different or uncomfortable.
  • A rough or unfamiliar texture: The tongue may detect the altered shape of the tooth.
  • Visible changes: The tooth beneath may appear discoloured, smaller, or altered in shape compared to surrounding teeth.
  • No discomfort at all: In some cases, particularly where the nerve has been treated previously (such as following root canal treatment), there may be little or no sensation.

It is worth noting that the absence of pain does not necessarily mean the situation does not require attention. A dentist can assess the state of the underlying tooth regardless of symptom presentation.

What to Do Immediately If Your Crown Comes Off

If your crown comes off, staying calm is the most helpful first step. There are several practical measures you can take whilst arranging to see your dentist:

  1. Retrieve and keep the crown. If possible, locate the crown and store it safely in a small, clean container. Do not discard it — your dentist may be able to re-cement it depending on its condition and the state of the underlying tooth.

  2. Do not attempt to permanently re-cement it yourself. Temporary dental adhesive products are available from pharmacies and may offer short-term comfort, but they are not a long-term solution and should not replace professional assessment.

  3. Be mindful of what you eat. Avoid very hard, sticky, or chewy foods on that side of the mouth until you have been seen by your dentist. Extreme temperatures may also heighten any sensitivity.

  4. Contact your dental practice. Explain the situation when you call. Most dental practices prioritise patients with dislodged crowns and will aim to see you as promptly as scheduling allows.

  5. Keep the area clean. Gently clean the exposed tooth with a soft toothbrush and avoid allowing food debris to accumulate around it.

When Professional Dental Assessment May Be Needed

Whilst a dislodged crown does not always constitute a dental emergency, there are situations in which prompt professional assessment is particularly advisable:

  • Significant or worsening pain: If the exposed tooth is causing increasing discomfort, especially spontaneous pain unrelated to eating or drinking, this warrants timely attention.
  • Swelling around the tooth or gum: Any visible swelling, whether around the tooth, the gum line, or the jaw area, should be assessed by a professional.
  • Signs of infection: These may include a persistent ache, a bad taste in the mouth, or a visible spot on the gum near the affected tooth.
  • The crown cannot be located: If the crown has been swallowed or lost, a replacement will need to be made following assessment.
  • The underlying tooth appears broken or damaged: If the tooth beneath the crown has fractured, this may affect the available treatment options and requires evaluation.
  • Prolonged sensitivity: Sensitivity that persists for several days or does not settle may indicate that the underlying tooth requires further assessment.

If you are experiencing any of these symptoms, seeking professional advice in a timely manner is always a reasonable course of action. A dentist can assess the situation fully and discuss the most appropriate next steps with you based on what the examination reveals. Treatment Options Your Dentist May Discuss

The treatment recommended following a dislodged crown will depend on what the clinical assessment reveals. Every patient's situation is different, and the dentist will consider several factors before advising on the most appropriate approach.

Possible treatment considerations may include:

  • Re-cementing the existing crown: If the crown is intact, the underlying tooth is in good condition, and the fit remains acceptable, re-cementing the crown may be a straightforward option.
  • A new crown: If the existing crown is damaged, or if changes to the tooth mean it no longer fits correctly, a replacement crown may be discussed. Learn more about dental crowns and how they work on our treatment pages.
  • Additional restoration work: If decay is found beneath the crown, this will typically need to be addressed before a new crown can be placed.
  • Root canal treatment: In cases where the nerve of the tooth has been affected, root canal treatment may be discussed before fitting a new crown.
  • Extraction: In rare cases where the underlying tooth is no longer restorable, extraction may be considered alongside discussion of replacement options.

Treatment suitability is always determined during a clinical examination. A dentist will not recommend a course of action without first assessing the tooth in person.

Prevention: Reducing the Risk of a Crown Coming Loose

Whilst no restoration lasts indefinitely, there are practical steps that may help to extend the lifespan of a dental crown and reduce the likelihood of it becoming dislodged:

  • Attend regular dental check-ups: Routine examinations allow your dentist to monitor the condition of existing restorations and identify any early signs of wear, decay, or fit issues before they become more significant.
  • Maintain a thorough oral hygiene routine: Brushing twice daily with fluoride toothpaste and cleaning between teeth daily helps to protect the margins of the crown where bacteria can accumulate.
  • Be mindful of hard or sticky foods: Chewing on very hard items such as ice, hard sweets, or crusty bread can place excessive force on crowns. Sticky foods may also pull at them over time.
  • Address teeth grinding (bruxism): If you grind or clench your teeth, particularly at night, speak to your dentist. A custom-made night guard may help to protect your restorations from excessive force.
  • Avoid using teeth as tools: Using teeth to open packaging, bite nails, or hold objects places unnecessary and uneven stress on restorations.
  • Seek advice promptly if a crown feels loose: Early intervention often simplifies treatment. If you notice that a crown feels slightly different or less secure, mentioning it at your next appointment — or contacting the practice sooner — is always sensible.

Good preventative dental care, including hygiene appointments, can play an important role in maintaining the health of both natural teeth and existing restorations.

Key Points to Remember

  • A dental crown coming off is a relatively common occurrence and is not always a dental emergency, but it does require prompt professional attention.
  • The exposed tooth beneath a crown is more vulnerable to sensitivity, bacteria, and further damage until a dentist has assessed and treated the situation.
  • Keep the crown safe and contact your dental practice as soon as possible following the incident.
  • Do not rely on over-the-counter adhesives as a long-term solution — they are a temporary measure only.
  • Treatment options depend entirely on the condition of the underlying tooth, which can only be assessed during a clinical examination.
  • Regular dental check-ups and a consistent oral hygiene routine may help to extend the life of existing crowns.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it a dental emergency if my crown comes off?

A dislodged crown is not always classified as a dental emergency, but it does require timely professional attention. If you are experiencing significant pain, swelling, or signs of infection, it is advisable to contact your dental practice promptly or seek urgent dental care. If you have no discomfort and the underlying tooth appears undamaged, your practice may still aim to see you within a short period to assess the situation and protect the exposed tooth from further risk.

Can I re-cement my crown at home?

Temporary dental adhesive products are available from pharmacies and can provide short-term comfort, but they are not designed to serve as a permanent solution. Attempting to permanently re-cement a crown at home is not advisable, as doing so without assessing the underlying tooth could mask decay, damage the tooth further, or result in a poor fit. Always seek a professional assessment to determine the most appropriate course of action for your individual circumstances.

How long can I leave a crown off before seeing a dentist?

It is generally advisable to contact your dental practice as soon as possible rather than waiting. The longer the underlying tooth remains exposed, the greater the risk of sensitivity, contamination, or further damage. Whilst the urgency will depend on your symptoms and the condition of the tooth, most dental professionals recommend seeking assessment within a day or two of the crown coming off, wherever possible.

Why did my crown come off in the first place?

There are several possible reasons a crown may come off, including natural deterioration of the dental cement over time, decay developing beneath the crown, physical force such as biting hard foods or grinding teeth, or changes in the fit of the crown due to alterations in the tooth structure. Your dentist will assess the specific circumstances during your appointment and explain the findings of their examination.

Will I need a completely new crown?

Not necessarily. Whether the existing crown can be re-cemented or whether a new one is required depends on the condition of the crown itself and the state of the underlying tooth. If the crown is intact and the tooth is in good condition, re-cementing may be suitable. If there is damage, decay, or a significant change in fit, a new crown may be recommended. This can only be determined following a clinical assessment.

Does a tooth still need a crown if the nerve has already been treated?

Yes. A tooth that has undergone root canal treatment often becomes more brittle over time and benefits from the protection of a crown. The crown helps to maintain the structural integrity of the tooth and prevent fracture. If the crown on a root canal-treated tooth comes off, it is particularly important to seek assessment promptly, as the tooth may be at greater risk without the protective restoration in place.

Conclusion

A dental crown coming off unexpectedly can feel alarming, but understanding what to do and why it happens can help you respond calmly and effectively. The key actions — retrieving the crown, keeping the area clean, avoiding hard foods, and contacting your dental practice — remain consistent regardless of whether you experience discomfort or not.

The exposed tooth beneath a dislodged crown is more vulnerable than a natural tooth and should be assessed professionally in a timely manner. Whether the outcome involves re-cementing the existing crown or exploring a new restoration, the appropriate path forward is always determined through clinical examination.

Taking good care of your oral health through regular check-ups, daily hygiene habits, and mindful eating can all contribute to the longevity of dental restorations. If a crown feels loose or has come off entirely, professional assessment is always the most appropriate next step.

Dental symptoms and treatment options should always be assessed individually during a clinical examination.
