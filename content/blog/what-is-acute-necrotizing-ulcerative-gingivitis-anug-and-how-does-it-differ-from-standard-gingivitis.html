Introduction

If you have noticed sudden, severe gum pain accompanied by an unpleasant taste or smell in your mouth, you may have searched online to understand what is happening. Many people are aware of common gingivitis — a mild form of gum disease — but far fewer have heard of Acute Necrotizing Ulcerative Gingivitis (ANUG), a more serious and distinct condition that requires prompt professional attention. If taste changes are part of your concern, this related guide on bitter taste when flossing may also be useful.

ANUG is a bacterial infection of the gums that presents with characteristic symptoms quite different from everyday gingivitis. Understanding the difference between the two conditions matters because they are managed differently, and delaying appropriate care for ANUG can allow the infection to progress.

This article explains what ANUG is, how it differs from standard gingivitis, what causes it, what symptoms to look out for, and what a dental professional may be able to do to help. If you are experiencing significant gum pain or other concerning symptoms, seeking a professional dental assessment is always advisable.

What Is Acute Necrotizing Ulcerative Gingivitis (ANUG)?

Acute Necrotizing Ulcerative Gingivitis (ANUG) is a serious bacterial infection of the gums characterised by painful ulceration, tissue death (necrosis), bleeding, and a distinctive foul odour. Unlike standard gingivitis, which causes inflammation without tissue destruction, ANUG involves actual necrosis of gum tissue and requires prompt professional dental treatment.

What Is Standard Gingivitis?

Gingivitis is the most common form of gum disease and refers to inflammation of the gingival tissue — the soft tissue that surrounds and supports the teeth. It is primarily caused by a build-up of dental plaque along the gumline. When plaque is not adequately removed through brushing and flossing, the bacteria within it trigger an inflammatory response in the gum tissue.

Standard gingivitis is considered a mild and reversible condition. It does not involve destruction of the deeper periodontal structures — such as the bone and ligaments that anchor teeth — unless it progresses to more advanced periodontitis.

Common signs of gingivitis include:

  • Redness or swelling of the gums
  • Bleeding when brushing or flossing
  • Mild tenderness
  • Slight puffiness along the gumline

Importantly, gingivitis is not typically painful at rest and does not cause ulceration or tissue death. With improved oral hygiene and professional cleaning, gingivitis often improves and may resolve. If you would like to understand more about the range of gum disease treatments available, a qualified dental professional can assess your individual situation and recommend appropriate care.

What Is Acute Necrotizing Ulcerative Gingivitis (ANUG)?

Acute Necrotizing Ulcerative Gingivitis (ANUG) is a specific and more severe bacterial infection of the gums. It is also sometimes referred to as "trench mouth," a name historically associated with soldiers in World War I who frequently developed the condition due to stress, poor nutrition, and inadequate oral hygiene.

ANUG is distinct from standard gingivitis in several important ways. It involves active tissue necrosis — meaning that gum tissue is being destroyed by the bacterial infection. This destruction typically begins at the tips of the interdental papillae (the pointed gum tissue between the teeth) and can spread if not treated.

Key features that characterise ANUG include:

  • Punched-out ulcers at the tips of the gum papillae
  • Necrosis (tissue death) of the affected gum tissue, which may appear grey or pseudomembranous
  • Severe and spontaneous pain, often described as sharp and constant
  • Profuse bleeding even without provocation
  • Halitosis (bad breath) that is notably strong and distinctive
  • Systemic features in some cases, including fever and general malaise

ANUG is not the same as periodontitis, though if left unmanaged it may progress to necrotizing ulcerative periodontitis (NUP), which involves deeper structures.

The Clinical Science Behind ANUG: What Is Happening in the Gums?

Understanding what is happening biologically helps explain why ANUG behaves so differently from standard gingivitis.

The infection is primarily driven by a group of bacteria known as fusospirochaetal organisms — a combination of Fusobacterium species and spirochaetes such as Treponema and Borrelia species. These bacteria are typically present in low numbers in the oral cavity as part of normal flora. However, under certain conditions — particularly when the immune system is compromised or when local resistance is reduced — they proliferate rapidly and begin to cause tissue destruction.

The bacterial toxins released by these organisms interfere with the blood supply to the gum tissue, causing localised ischaemia (reduced blood flow) and ultimately necrosis. The characteristic grey pseudomembrane seen on affected gum tissue is composed of dead cells, fibrin, and bacteria.

This pathological process is fundamentally different from the inflammatory but non-destructive mechanism seen in standard gingivitis, which is why ANUG cannot simply be managed with improved oral hygiene alone — professional intervention is an important part of appropriate management.

What Causes ANUG? Risk Factors and Predisposing Conditions

ANUG does not typically occur in otherwise healthy individuals with good oral hygiene. Several predisposing factors are commonly associated with its development:

Immune System Compromise

Conditions that reduce immune function — including HIV infection, poorly controlled diabetes, and malnutrition — are associated with increased susceptibility to ANUG. In some cases, a diagnosis of ANUG may prompt investigation into underlying systemic health.

Psychological Stress

Psychological stress is one of the most frequently cited risk factors. Stress can suppress immune function through the release of cortisol and other stress hormones, lowering the body's local resistance to oral bacteria.

Poor Oral Hygiene

Pre-existing plaque accumulation and existing gingivitis create an environment in which the bacteria responsible for ANUG can more easily thrive.

Tobacco Use

Smoking and other forms of tobacco use are recognised risk factors, as tobacco affects gingival blood flow and immune response.

Nutritional Deficiency

Deficiencies in vitamins C and B complex have historically been associated with increased vulnerability, though this is less commonly a sole causative factor in contemporary UK populations.

Recent Illness or Viral Infection

Viral infections, particularly those affecting the upper respiratory tract, may temporarily reduce mucosal resistance, creating favourable conditions for bacterial overgrowth.

Understanding these risk factors can help individuals take steps to reduce their vulnerability. For personalised guidance on maintaining good oral health and gum health, a dental professional can provide advice tailored to your circumstances.

How ANUG Differs from Standard Gingivitis: A Comparison

Whilst both conditions affect the gum tissue, the differences between ANUG and standard gingivitis are clinically significant:

Feature Standard Gingivitis ANUG
Onset Gradual Rapid (acute)
Pain Mild or absent Severe and spontaneous
Tissue destruction None Yes — necrosis of gum papillae
Ulceration Absent Characteristic punched-out ulcers
Bleeding Provoked (e.g., brushing) Spontaneous and profuse
Odour Mild or none Distinctive and strong
Systemic symptoms Absent Possible — fever, malaise
Reversibility Fully reversible Requires professional treatment; scarring possible
Bacteria involved Mixed plaque biofilm Fusospirochaetal organisms

This comparison highlights why accurate identification of the condition by a qualified dental professional is important. The two conditions, whilst both involving the gum tissue, require different approaches to management.

Symptoms of ANUG: When Should You Be Concerned?

The symptoms of ANUG typically develop rapidly and are usually noticeable enough to prompt individuals to seek dental advice. The most commonly reported symptoms include, and these can overlap with signs discussed in this article about bleeding gums when flossing:

Gum Pain Pain is usually one of the earliest and most distressing symptoms. It tends to be continuous rather than intermittent, and may be described as a burning or throbbing sensation.

Bleeding Gums Spontaneous gingival bleeding — not just when brushing — is a common feature. The gums may bleed when touched or even at rest.

Distinctive Odour A particularly strong, unpleasant oral odour that is difficult to mask with mouthwash or breath fresheners is characteristic of ANUG. This reflects the presence of the specific bacteria involved.

Ulceration Crater-like ulcers at the tips of the interdental papillae are a hallmark feature. These may appear to have a grey, necrotic covering.

Altered Taste A metallic or unpleasant taste in the mouth is frequently reported.

Systemic Symptoms In some cases, particularly where the infection is more widespread or the individual is immunocompromised, symptoms may include mild fever, fatigue, and swollen lymph nodes in the neck.

If you are experiencing a combination of these symptoms, it is advisable to seek dental assessment promptly rather than waiting to see if symptoms resolve on their own.

When Professional Dental Assessment May Be Needed

If you are experiencing any of the following, seeking a professional dental assessment is recommended:

  • Sudden onset of severe gum pain that is persistent and does not ease with over-the-counter pain relief
  • Spontaneous gum bleeding that is not related to brushing or eating
  • Visible ulceration or grey patches on the gum tissue between the teeth
  • A strong and persistent unpleasant oral odour that develops suddenly
  • Swollen lymph nodes in the jaw or neck area alongside gum symptoms
  • Fever or feeling unwell in combination with gum symptoms
  • Worsening symptoms despite improved oral hygiene

It is important to note that this article cannot provide a diagnosis. The symptoms described above can be associated with various dental and medical conditions, and only a qualified dental professional can evaluate your situation through a clinical examination. Prompt assessment helps ensure that an appropriate management plan can be developed without unnecessary delay.

How Is ANUG Managed by Dental Professionals?

Whilst this article does not aim to outline a specific treatment protocol, it is helpful to have a general understanding of the approaches a dental professional may use when managing ANUG.

Professional Debridement

A gentle but thorough removal of necrotic tissue and bacterial deposits by a dental professional is typically a core component of management. This is undertaken carefully, with appropriate pain management, to reduce the bacterial load and encourage healing.

Antimicrobial Therapy

Antibiotics — most commonly metronidazole — are frequently prescribed in cases of ANUG. The choice and duration of antibiotic therapy are determined by the clinician based on the severity of the condition, the patient's medical history, and any contraindications. Antibiotics should only be prescribed by a registered healthcare professional.

Chlorhexidine Mouthwash

Antimicrobial mouthwash, such as chlorhexidine gluconate, is often recommended as an adjunct to other treatment. It helps reduce the oral bacterial load whilst the gum tissue is healing.

Pain Management

Appropriate analgesics may be recommended to manage discomfort during the acute phase.

Oral Hygiene Instruction and Support

As healing progresses, guidance on effective oral hygiene techniques helps prevent recurrence and supports long-term gum health.

Investigation of Underlying Factors

Where appropriate, the dental professional may discuss risk factors and, in some cases, liaise with a patient's medical practitioner if an underlying systemic condition may be contributory.

Prevention and Oral Health Advice

Whilst ANUG can occur even with reasonable oral hygiene in the context of significant risk factors, there are several practical steps that may help reduce vulnerability:

Maintain Consistent Oral Hygiene Brushing twice daily with a fluoride toothpaste, cleaning between the teeth daily with interdental brushes or floss, and using an appropriate mouthwash helps keep the gum environment less hospitable to harmful bacteria.

Attend Regular Dental Check-ups Regular dental examinations allow a professional to identify early signs of gum disease and address them before they worsen. The frequency of check-ups appropriate for you will be guided by your dental professional.

Manage Stress Where Possible Given the association between psychological stress and ANUG, exploring stress management strategies — whether through exercise, mindfulness, or professional support — may have benefits for overall health as well as oral health.

Avoid or Reduce Tobacco Use Smoking is a known risk factor for both ANUG and broader gum disease. Reducing or stopping tobacco use supports gum health and overall wellbeing. Your GP or dental team can provide information about available support.

Maintain a Balanced Diet Adequate nutrition, including sufficient vitamins and minerals, supports immune function and gum tissue health.

Seek Timely Dental Care If you notice early signs of gum problems — such as persistent bleeding, swelling, or discomfort — seeking dental advice promptly may prevent more significant problems from developing.

Key Points to Remember

  • ANUG is not the same as standard gingivitis — it is a more serious bacterial infection involving tissue destruction (necrosis) and requires professional dental management.
  • Key distinguishing features of ANUG include severe spontaneous pain, crater-like ulcers between the teeth, profuse bleeding, and a distinctive oral odour.
  • Risk factors include stress, smoking, immune compromise, poor oral hygiene, and nutritional deficiency.
  • Standard gingivitis is a reversible inflammatory condition that can often be resolved with improved oral hygiene and professional cleaning.
  • Early professional assessment is important if ANUG symptoms are suspected, as prompt treatment supports healing and helps prevent progression.
  • Prevention involves consistent oral hygiene, regular dental check-ups, a balanced diet, and lifestyle factors such as stress management and tobacco reduction.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is ANUG contagious?

Current evidence does not indicate that ANUG is directly contagious in the same way as, for example, a respiratory viral infection. However, the bacteria involved are transmissible to a degree, so practising good general hygiene — such as not sharing utensils, toothbrushes, or cups — is sensible. ANUG typically develops when predisposing factors are present in an individual, rather than being acquired purely through contact. A dental professional can answer any specific questions about transmission and hygiene precautions.

Can ANUG go away on its own without treatment?

It is generally not advisable to leave suspected ANUG without professional assessment. Whilst minor symptoms of gum irritation may improve with better oral hygiene, ANUG involves active tissue destruction and bacterial infection that typically requires professional debridement and, in many cases, antibiotic therapy. Leaving it unmanaged may allow the infection to progress and could result in more significant gum damage. If you are experiencing symptoms consistent with ANUG, seeking dental advice promptly is recommended.

How long does it take to recover from ANUG with treatment?

With appropriate professional management, many patients begin to experience improvement within a few days of starting treatment. Complete resolution of symptoms and healing of gum tissue may take one to two weeks or longer, depending on the severity of the episode and the individual's overall health. Following all professional advice, maintaining good oral hygiene, and attending any follow-up appointments supports the recovery process. Individual recovery experiences will vary, and a clinician can give a more specific indication based on your presentation.

Can ANUG come back after treatment?

Yes, ANUG can recur, particularly if the underlying predisposing factors — such as stress, smoking, or poor oral hygiene — are not addressed. Following treatment, a dental professional will typically provide oral hygiene guidance and discuss risk factor modification to reduce the likelihood of recurrence. Attending follow-up appointments and ongoing dental check-ups supports long-term gum health and helps identify any early signs of recurrence promptly.

Is ANUG the same as periodontitis?

No, ANUG and periodontitis are distinct conditions, although they can be related. ANUG primarily affects the superficial gum tissue (gingiva), whereas periodontitis involves destruction of the deeper supporting structures of the teeth, including the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone. If ANUG is not appropriately managed, it can progress to necrotizing ulcerative periodontitis (NUP), which does involve these deeper structures. Both conditions require professional dental management, and their assessment and treatment are guided by clinical examination.

Should I see a dentist or a doctor if I suspect ANUG?

Your dental team is usually the most appropriate first point of contact for suspected ANUG, as the condition is primarily an oral condition requiring dental assessment and management. However, if you are also experiencing significant systemic symptoms such as high fever, severe difficulty swallowing, or you are aware of an underlying health condition that may be relevant, informing both your dentist and your GP allows for coordinated care. In cases of significant swelling or difficulty breathing, urgent medical attention should be sought immediately.

Conclusion

Acute Necrotizing Ulcerative Gingivitis (ANUG) is a distinct and more serious condition than standard gingivitis, characterised by rapid onset, severe pain, tissue necrosis, characteristic ulceration, and a strong oral odour. Whilst standard gingivitis is a common and reversible form of gum inflammation primarily driven by plaque accumulation, ANUG involves a specific bacterial infection that causes actual destruction of gum tissue and typically requires professional dental treatment.

Understanding the key differences between these two conditions can help individuals recognise when their symptoms may warrant prompt dental attention rather than simply improved home oral hygiene. Risk factors such as stress, smoking, immune compromise, and nutritional status play an important role in vulnerability to ANUG, making lifestyle and general health considerations relevant alongside dental care.

If you are experiencing symptoms that may be consistent with ANUG — or if you have concerns about your gum health more generally — seeking a professional dental assessment is the most appropriate course of action.

Dental symptoms and treatment options should always be assessed individually during a clinical examination.
