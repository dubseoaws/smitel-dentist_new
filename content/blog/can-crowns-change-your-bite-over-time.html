Introduction

Many patients who have had a dental crown fitted find themselves wondering, sometime later, whether their bite feels slightly different to how it once did. This is a genuinely common concern, and it is entirely reasonable to question whether a crown — a prosthetic restoration designed to protect and rebuild a damaged tooth — could gradually affect the way your upper and lower teeth meet.

People often search online after noticing subtle changes such as jaw discomfort, difficulty chewing, or a new sensitivity when biting down. Understanding the relationship between dental crowns and bite changes can help you make sense of what you might be experiencing and, importantly, when it may be worth speaking to a dental professional.

This article explores how crowns interact with your bite, why bite shifts can occur, what symptoms to be aware of, and what steps you can take to maintain good oral health after crown placement. All information here is educational; individual concerns should always be assessed by a qualified dentist.

Can Dental Crowns Change Your Bite?

Yes, dental crowns can influence your bite, both shortly after placement and over longer periods. A crown that is even marginally too high, or one that has shifted slightly due to wear, cement breakdown, or changes in surrounding teeth, may alter how your teeth meet. This is known as bite change or occlusal change, and it warrants professional dental review.

What Is a Dental Crown and How Does It Work?

A dental crown is a custom-made cap that fits over a prepared natural tooth or a dental implant. Crowns are commonly used to restore teeth that are cracked, significantly decayed, or have undergone root canal treatment. They may be made from porcelain, ceramic, metal alloy, or a combination of materials.

Once cemented in place, a crown is designed to replicate the shape, size, and function of your natural tooth. The crown must be carefully adjusted so that it integrates seamlessly with the rest of your bite — the technical term for this is occlusion.

When a crown is first fitted, your dentist will check your occlusion by asking you to bite on articulating paper (a thin, coloured sheet that marks where your teeth make contact). This allows for precise adjustments before you leave the practice.

However, teeth are living structures embedded in bone and surrounded by soft tissue. The mouth is a dynamic environment, and it changes over time. This is why even a well-fitted crown may, in some circumstances, begin to feel different months or years after placement. Understanding this can help patients recognise when a routine review might be beneficial.

How Can a Crown Affect Your Bite Over Time?

Bite change after crown placement is not inevitable, but it is a recognised possibility. Several mechanisms can contribute to gradual occlusal shifts.

1. Normal Wear of Crown Material Over years of chewing, crown materials — particularly porcelain — can wear down at a different rate to natural tooth enamel. This uneven wear can subtly alter the height and contact points of the crown, which in turn may shift how opposing or neighbouring teeth sit.

2. Cement Failure or Marginal Changes The adhesive cement holding a crown in place can degrade over time, particularly if exposed to repeated moisture, grinding forces, or microleakage. Even minimal loosening can cause tiny shifts in crown position that affect occlusion.

3. Changes in Surrounding Teeth Teeth adjacent to or opposite a crown are still subject to movement, wear, and gum recession. If a neighbouring tooth drifts slightly, or if gum tissue recedes, the overall bite can change in ways that make the crown feel higher or lower than it once did.

4. Jaw Changes and Bone Remodelling Subtle changes in the jawbone and temporomandibular joint (TMJ) can occur throughout adulthood, particularly in patients who grind or clench their teeth (a condition known as bruxism). These changes can affect the overall bite pattern, making an otherwise well-fitted crown feel misaligned.

The Dental Science Behind Bite and Occlusion

To understand why crowns can influence your bite, it helps to understand the science of occlusion — how your upper and lower teeth come together.

Your bite is not static. It is maintained through a complex balance between your teeth, the periodontal ligament (the tissue that cushions each tooth within its socket), your jaw muscles, and the temporomandibular joints on either side of your face. Every tooth plays a role in distributing the forces generated during chewing, speaking, and swallowing.

When a crown is placed, it becomes a new element within this balanced system. If the crown's surface contacts the opposing tooth even slightly more than the natural tooth did, the surrounding structures must adapt. The periodontal ligament may compress or stretch; the jaw muscles may alter their firing patterns to accommodate the new contact.

Over time, if a high bite is not corrected, compensatory changes can develop. Equally, if crown material wears away, a low bite can develop, potentially causing adjacent teeth to over-erupt (drift upward or downward to fill the gap in contact). Both scenarios illustrate why occlusal balance is so important to the long-term success of a crown — and to your overall dental health.

Signs That Your Crown May Be Affecting Your Bite

Bite changes following crown placement do not always produce obvious symptoms immediately. Some patients notice changes weeks after fitting; others only become aware of issues years later. The following signs may suggest your bite requires professional review:

  • Discomfort or pain when biting or chewing, particularly on the crowned tooth
  • Jaw aching or fatigue, especially after eating or upon waking
  • Sensitivity in the crowned tooth or neighbouring teeth, without an obvious cause
  • Clicking or popping sounds in the jaw joint
  • Headaches, particularly around the temples, which can sometimes be associated with bite changes
  • Uneven wear on other teeth, which your dentist may observe at a routine check-up
  • A feeling that your teeth do not fit together as they used to

It is important to note that these symptoms can have multiple causes. A bite change is one possibility, but sensitivity and jaw discomfort may also be related to other dental or medical factors. This is why a thorough clinical assessment is always the appropriate first step.

When Should You Seek Professional Dental Assessment?

You do not need to wait until symptoms become severe before speaking to your dentist. In fact, routine dental check-ups are an effective way to detect early bite changes before they develop into more significant problems.

That said, certain situations make it particularly appropriate to book an appointment sooner rather than later:

  • Persistent discomfort on biting that has lasted more than a few days after crown placement
  • Pain or aching in the jaw that is new or worsening
  • Sensitivity that is spreading to neighbouring teeth
  • Visible changes in your teeth, such as chipping, cracking, or unusual wear patterns
  • Any looseness or movement of the crown itself
  • Symptoms affecting your sleep or daily activities, such as jaw clenching at night or difficulty eating comfortably

A dentist will examine your bite using clinical tests and may take X-rays or use occlusal analysis tools to assess how your teeth are meeting. Based on this assessment, they can determine whether the crown requires adjustment, replacement, or whether another cause is responsible for your symptoms.

It is always helpful to raise concerns early. Minor bite adjustments can often be straightforward when identified promptly, though complexity varies depending on individual clinical circumstances. Unaddressed occlusal issues, however, can place additional stress on teeth, the jaw joint, and surrounding soft tissue over time.

How Crowns Are Adjusted and What to Expect

If your dentist confirms that a crown is sitting too high or too low relative to your bite, an adjustment can usually be made during a clinical appointment. This process is generally well-tolerated and relatively quick, though individual experiences may vary.

Bite adjustment (also called occlusal equilibration) involves the dentist using articulating paper to identify the precise point of excess contact. They will then carefully reduce the crown material using a fine dental bur until the bite feels balanced and even pressure is distributed across multiple teeth.

In some cases — for example, where a crown has worn significantly, developed a crack, or where cement failure has occurred — a replacement crown may be the most appropriate course of action. Your dentist will discuss all available options with you based on their clinical findings. A full treatment plan, including an itemised breakdown of associated costs, will be provided prior to any treatment commencing. Fees may vary depending on individual clinical requirements.

It is worth noting that bite adjustment does not affect the overall appearance of a crown in most cases. The modification is typically limited to the biting surface, and the aesthetic result remains largely unchanged.

For patients who grind their teeth, an occlusal splint (night guard) may be recommended alongside any crown adjustment to protect the restoration from further wear as part of your broader oral health management.

Prevention and Oral Health Advice for Crown Patients

Whilst it is not always possible to prevent all bite changes, there are practical steps that can help crown patients maintain good occlusal health over the long term.

Attend regular dental check-ups. A reliable way to detect early bite changes is through consistent professional monitoring. Your dentist can identify subtle shifts before they become symptomatic.

Inform your dentist about grinding or clenching. Bruxism places significant additional force on crowns and natural teeth alike. If you are aware that you grind your teeth, this is important information for your dental team to know when planning and reviewing restorations.

Wear a night guard if one has been recommended. Occlusal splints are specifically designed to protect crowns and natural teeth from the damaging effects of nocturnal grinding.

Avoid habits that place uneven pressure on your teeth. Chewing on ice, nail biting, and using your teeth as tools can all contribute to crown wear and bite imbalance.

Report any new symptoms promptly. You do not need to wait for your next scheduled check-up if something feels different. Contacting your dental practice to describe your symptoms is always appropriate.

Maintain excellent oral hygiene around the crown. Although crowns themselves cannot decay, the natural tooth structure beneath them can. Plaque accumulation at the crown margin can lead to gum recession, which may indirectly affect how the crown sits.

Key Points to Remember

  • Dental crowns can influence your bite, both immediately after placement and gradually over time.
  • Common causes include crown wear, cement changes, shifting of adjacent teeth, and jaw changes related to bruxism or ageing.
  • Symptoms such as bite discomfort, jaw aching, and sensitivity may indicate that a bite assessment is warranted.
  • Minor bite adjustments can often be straightforward when identified early through regular dental check-ups, though outcomes depend on individual clinical circumstances.
  • Bruxism significantly increases the risk of crown wear and bite changes; a night guard can help protect restorations.
  • Treatment suitability and the need for any adjustment or replacement depends entirely on individual clinical assessment.

Frequently Asked Questions

How soon after getting a crown can bite problems develop?

Bite issues can arise very shortly after crown placement — sometimes within days. It is not uncommon for patients to notice that a new crown feels slightly high when they first bite together. In many cases, minor discomfort settles as the mouth adapts, but if it persists beyond a week or causes significant pain, the crown should be reviewed and adjusted. Longer-term changes can develop gradually over months or years due to crown wear or shifts in surrounding teeth.

Is it normal for a new crown to feel different when biting?

Some degree of adjustment is normal in the first few days after a crown is placed. Your mouth is highly sensitive to even very small changes in tooth height, and it can take a short time to adapt to a new restoration. However, if the crown consistently feels noticeably higher than your other teeth, or if biting causes sharp pain or significant discomfort, you should contact your dentist. This is not something to simply tolerate, as an unaddressed high bite can place unnecessary stress on the crown and jaw.

Can a bite problem caused by a crown damage other teeth?

Yes, over time an uncorrected bite imbalance can contribute to uneven wear on natural teeth and other restorations. Teeth that are taking more force than they are designed to handle may become worn, chipped, or more sensitive. Additionally, persistent occlusal issues can place strain on the temporomandibular joint, potentially contributing to jaw discomfort and muscle tension. This is why early assessment is beneficial — small adjustments made promptly can help prevent wider dental health consequences, though individual outcomes will depend on clinical assessment.

Can teeth grinding cause crown problems?

Bruxism — the habitual grinding or clenching of teeth — is one of the more significant risk factors for crown problems. The forces generated during grinding are considerably greater than those of normal chewing, and they can accelerate crown wear, weaken the cement bond, and contribute to bite changes over time. Patients who grind their teeth are often advised to wear a custom occlusal splint, particularly at night, to protect their restorations and reduce the risk of long-term damage.

How long should a dental crown last?

With appropriate care and regular dental monitoring, dental crowns can last many years — often well over a decade. However, longevity depends on a number of factors including the material used, the patient's oral hygiene, dietary habits, and whether there is any history of grinding. Regular check-ups allow your dentist to assess the condition of your crown over time and advise on whether any intervention is needed. There is no universal guarantee of lifespan, as individual circumstances vary considerably.

Will crown adjustment change the appearance of my tooth?

In most cases, bite adjustment involves only minor refinements to the biting surface of a crown and does not significantly alter its visible appearance. The aim of adjustment is to reduce excess contact points rather than reshape the tooth cosmetically. If a crown has worn considerably and requires replacement, your dentist will discuss how the new restoration will look as part of the treatment planning process, ensuring that both function and aesthetics are considered.

Conclusion

Bite changes related to dental crowns are a well-recognised occurrence in restorative dentistry. Whether caused by natural wear, cement degradation, shifts in adjacent teeth, or the effects of bruxism, dental crown bite changes can develop gradually and may not always produce obvious symptoms in the early stages. Understanding the signs — such as jaw discomfort, new sensitivity, or a feeling that your teeth no longer meet as they should — can help you seek timely professional advice.

The good news is that many bite-related issues with crowns can be straightforward to address when identified early. Regular dental check-ups remain a valuable tool for monitoring the health and function of existing restorations. If you have concerns about your crown or your bite, speaking to your dentist is always the most appropriate course of action.
