What Happens If You Don't Get a Root Canal?

When a tooth with an infected or damaged pulp is left untreated, the infection does not typically resolve on its own. Over time, bacteria may continue to spread within and beyond the tooth, potentially leading to a dental abscess, bone loss, and further complications. A root canal vs doing nothing is not simply a choice between treatment and waiting â€” it is often a choice between preserving the tooth and risking its loss. Professional assessment is essential to determine the best approach for each individual case.

Why Might a Root Canal Be Recommended?

Root canal treatment, also known as endodontic treatment, is typically recommended when the soft tissue inside a tooth â€” called the dental pulp â€” becomes infected or irreversibly inflamed. This can occur for several reasons, including:

  • Deep dental decay that has reached the inner pulp chamber
  • A crack or fracture in the tooth allowing bacteria to enter
  • Repeated dental procedures on the same tooth causing cumulative damage
  • Trauma or injury to the tooth, even if there is no visible crack

The pulp contains nerves, blood vessels, and connective tissue. When it becomes compromised, the body's immune system may struggle to clear the infection effectively because of the limited blood supply within the root canal system.

It is important to understand that a recommendation for root canal treatment is based on clinical findings, including symptoms, dental X-rays, and examination results. Not every toothache requires a root canal, and not every tooth with decay will progress to the point of needing one. Your dentist will assess your individual situation before recommending any course of action.

Understanding Tooth Anatomy and the Infection Process

To appreciate what happens when a tooth infection is left untreated, it helps to understand basic tooth anatomy.

Each tooth has several layers:

  • Enamel â€” the hard, protective outer covering
  • Dentine â€” a softer layer beneath the enamel that makes up most of the tooth structure
  • Pulp â€” the innermost chamber containing nerves and blood vessels
  • Root canals â€” narrow channels running from the pulp chamber down through the roots to the surrounding bone

When bacteria penetrate through decay or damage and reach the pulp, they can multiply within this enclosed space. Because the pulp has a limited ability to heal itself, the infection often progresses rather than resolves.

Over time, bacteria may travel through the root canals and exit through the tip of the root (the apex), entering the surrounding jawbone. This can lead to the formation of a periapical abscess â€” a pocket of infection at the root tip. The bone around the affected area may gradually break down, which is often visible on dental X-rays as a dark shadow around the root.

This process can develop slowly over weeks, months, or even years, and it does not always cause constant pain, which is why some patients may be unaware of the progression.

What May Happen If an Infected Tooth Is Left Untreated

When considering root canal vs doing nothing, it is helpful to understand the potential timeline of an untreated dental infection. While every case varies, the following is a general overview of what may occur:

Early Stage

The tooth may feel sensitive to hot or cold temperatures, or there may be mild, intermittent discomfort. Some patients report no symptoms at all during this phase. The infection may be contained within the pulp chamber.

Intermediate Stage

As the infection progresses, pain may become more persistent or severe. There may be sensitivity when biting or chewing. The tooth may feel slightly loose, and the surrounding gum tissue could become tender or swollen.

Advanced Stage

Without intervention, the infection may spread beyond the tooth into the jawbone and surrounding tissues. A visible swelling or a small pimple-like bump (known as a sinus tract or gum boil) may appear on the gum near the affected tooth. In some cases, patients experience intermittent flare-ups followed by periods of reduced discomfort, which can create a misleading impression that the problem is improving.

Potential Long-Term Consequences

Over an extended period, untreated infection may lead to:

  • Significant bone loss around the tooth root
  • Spread of infection to adjacent teeth
  • Formation of a dental cyst
  • Tooth loss

In rare but serious cases, a dental infection can spread more widely, requiring urgent medical attention.

Why Pain May Come and Go â€” and Why That Doesn't Mean the Problem Has Resolved

One of the most common misconceptions about dental infections is that if the pain stops, the problem has gone away. This is an understandable assumption, but it is not clinically accurate in most cases.

When the nerve within the pulp dies due to infection, the tooth may temporarily stop sending pain signals. This can feel like relief, but the underlying infection typically continues to develop. The bacteria remain active within the root canal system and may continue to spread into the surrounding bone.

Periods of reduced pain can alternate with acute flare-ups, often triggered by stress, illness, or changes in the immune system. Each flare-up may cause the infection to extend further.

This is one reason why regular dental check-ups are so valuable â€” they allow your dentist to monitor teeth that may be at risk and identify problems before they become more complex to manage.

What Does Root Canal Treatment Involve?

Understanding the treatment itself can help address concerns and anxieties. Root canal treatment is a well-established procedure designed to remove infected tissue from inside the tooth while preserving the outer structure.

The general steps include:

  1. Local anaesthesia is administered to ensure comfort throughout the procedure
  2. Access is created through the top of the tooth to reach the pulp chamber
  3. Infected pulp tissue is carefully removed from the chamber and root canals
  4. The canals are cleaned, shaped, and disinfected to eliminate bacteria
  5. The canals are filled with a biocompatible material to seal them
  6. The tooth is restored, usually with a crown or permanent filling, to protect it and restore function

Modern root canal treatment is carried out with precision instruments and techniques. Most patients report that the procedure is comparable to having a filling placed, and the discomfort associated with the infection often reduces significantly following treatment.

When Should You Seek a Professional Dental Assessment?

There are several situations where it may be advisable to arrange a dental appointment. These include:

  • Persistent or worsening toothache that lasts more than a day or two
  • Sensitivity to hot or cold that lingers after the stimulus is removed
  • Pain when biting or chewing on a particular tooth
  • Swelling in the gum, jaw, or face
  • A small bump or pimple on the gum near a tooth
  • Darkening or discolouration of a tooth
  • A previously treated tooth that has started causing discomfort again

If you experience significant facial swelling, difficulty swallowing, or fever alongside dental pain, it is important to seek prompt medical or dental attention, as these may indicate a more serious infection.

These symptoms do not automatically mean you need a root canal â€” only a thorough clinical examination and appropriate diagnostic imaging can determine the right course of action. However, early assessment often provides more treatment options and may lead to better outcomes.

Prevention and Maintaining Good Oral Health

While not all causes of pulp damage can be prevented, there are practical steps that may help reduce the risk of dental infections:

  • Brush twice daily with a fluoride toothpaste, taking care to clean along the gum line
  • Clean between your teeth daily using interdental brushes or floss
  • Attend regular dental check-ups so that decay and damage can be identified early
  • Limit sugary foods and acidic drinks, particularly between meals
  • Wear a mouthguard during contact sports to reduce the risk of dental trauma
  • Address dental problems promptly â€” a small filling today may prevent a larger problem later

Maintaining a good relationship with your dental practice and attending routine appointments allows your dentist to monitor your oral health over time and intervene early when necessary. Prevention and early treatment are almost always simpler and more comfortable than managing advanced dental problems.

  • Treatment suitability depends on individual clinical assessment

Frequently Asked Questions

Is root canal treatment painful?

Modern root canal treatment is performed under local anaesthesia, which means the area is fully numbed before the procedure begins. Most patients find the treatment itself no more uncomfortable than having a filling. The discomfort people often associate with root canals is usually caused by the underlying infection rather than the treatment. Following the procedure, some mild tenderness is normal for a few days and can typically be managed with over-the-counter pain relief as advised by your dentist.

Can an infected tooth heal on its own without treatment?

In most cases, once the dental pulp becomes infected, it cannot heal without professional intervention. The enclosed nature of the root canal system limits the body's ability to fight the infection effectively. While symptoms may sometimes diminish temporarily â€” particularly if the nerve dies â€” the underlying bacterial infection typically continues. Regular dental assessments help monitor at-risk teeth and identify infections that may not be causing obvious symptoms.

How long can you leave a tooth that needs a root canal?

There is no fixed timeframe, as each situation is different. Some infections progress slowly over months or years, while others can develop more rapidly. Delaying treatment generally allows the infection more time to spread, which may make eventual treatment more complex or reduce the likelihood of saving the tooth. If your dentist has recommended root canal treatment, it is advisable to discuss the timing and any concerns you may have so that an appropriate plan can be made.

What are the alternatives to root canal treatment?

The main alternative to root canal treatment is extraction â€” removing the tooth entirely. While this eliminates the source of infection, it also means losing the natural tooth, which may then need to be replaced with an implant, bridge, or denture to restore function and appearance. In some very early cases, other approaches may be considered, but these depend on the specific clinical findings. Your dentist will explain all suitable options during your consultation.

Can antibiotics cure a tooth infection without a root canal?

Antibiotics may be prescribed to help manage the spread of a dental infection, particularly if there is significant swelling or systemic symptoms. However, antibiotics alone do not typically eliminate the source of infection within the tooth. The bacteria reside inside the root canal system, which has a limited blood supply, making it difficult for antibiotics to reach the area effectively. Antibiotics are generally used as a supportive measure alongside dental treatment rather than as a standalone solution.

How much does root canal treatment cost privately in the UK?

The cost of root canal treatment varies depending on several factors, including the tooth involved, the complexity of the root canal system, and the type of restoration required afterwards. Front teeth typically have simpler root systems and may cost less than molars, which have multiple roots. During your consultation, your dentist will provide a clear treatment plan and cost breakdown so that you can make an informed decision. Many practices also offer payment plans to help manage costs.
