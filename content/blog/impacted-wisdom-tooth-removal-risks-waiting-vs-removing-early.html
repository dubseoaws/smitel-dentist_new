What Is an Impacted Wisdom Tooth?

Wisdom teeth â€” also known as third molars â€” are the last teeth to develop, typically appearing between the ages of 17 and 25. Most adults have four wisdom teeth, one in each corner of the mouth, though some people may have fewer or none at all.

A wisdom tooth is described as "impacted" when it is unable to fully emerge through the gum into its expected position. This usually happens because there isn't enough space in the jaw to accommodate the tooth, or because the tooth is growing at an angle that prevents normal eruption.

What Are the Different Types of Impaction?

The way a wisdom tooth is positioned beneath the gum determines the type of impaction. The most common types include:

  • Mesial (angled forward): The tooth is tilted towards the front of the mouth, pressing against the second molar. This is the most frequently seen type of impaction.
  • Vertical: The tooth is in a relatively normal upright position but cannot fully break through the gum due to lack of space.
  • Horizontal: The tooth is lying on its side, completely sideways within the jawbone. This type can sometimes exert pressure on the roots of neighbouring teeth.
  • Distal (angled backward): The tooth is tilted towards the back of the mouth, away from the adjacent molar.

In addition, impacted wisdom teeth can be classified as:

  • Soft tissue impaction: The tooth has emerged through the bone but remains partially covered by gum tissue.
  • Partial bony impaction: The tooth has partially erupted but part of it remains embedded in the jawbone.
  • Full bony impaction: The tooth is completely encased within the jawbone and has not broken through at all.

Why Do Wisdom Teeth Often Struggle to Erupt Fully?

The most common reason is simply a lack of space. Over the course of human evolution, jaw sizes have gradually reduced, but the number of teeth we develop has remained the same. As a result, the last teeth to arrive â€” the wisdom teeth â€” often find there is insufficient room for them to emerge properly.

Other contributing factors may include the angle of the tooth's development, the density of the surrounding bone, and the position of adjacent teeth. Each case is unique, which is why a clinical assessment including X-rays is typically needed to understand the specific situation.

Common Symptoms of an Impacted Wisdom Tooth

Some impacted wisdom teeth cause no noticeable symptoms and are only identified during routine dental examinations or X-rays. However, when symptoms do develop, they typically affect the area at the very back of the mouth and may include:

Pain and Discomfort

  • Aching or throbbing pain at the back of the jaw, which may come and go or become persistent
  • Tenderness or soreness in the gum tissue surrounding the partially erupted tooth
  • Pain that may radiate to the ear, temple, or neighbouring teeth
  • Discomfort when opening the mouth wide or when chewing food

Signs That May Suggest Infection

When gum tissue partially covers an impacted wisdom tooth, it can create a pocket where food debris and bacteria accumulate. This may lead to an infection known as pericoronitis. Signs that may suggest infection include:

  • Redness and swelling of the gum around the wisdom tooth
  • An unpleasant taste in the mouth or persistent bad breath
  • Difficulty chewing or biting without discomfort
  • Pus or discharge around the affected area
  • Swollen lymph nodes in the neck
  • In some cases, a mild fever or general feeling of being unwell

Other Possible Indicators

  • Jaw stiffness or difficulty opening the mouth fully
  • Swelling in the cheek or jaw area
  • Headaches that seem to originate from the jaw area
  • Changes in bite alignment if the wisdom tooth is pressing on other teeth

Worth noting: The presence of symptoms doesn't automatically mean removal is required, and the absence of symptoms doesn't always mean everything is fine. Regular dental check-ups allow your dentist to monitor wisdom teeth over time and identify any changes that may need attention.

The Risks of Waiting Too Long

When an impacted wisdom tooth is causing problems â€” or clinical assessment suggests it is likely to cause problems â€” delaying treatment may allow the situation to become more complex over time. While every case is different, and not all impacted wisdom teeth will cause complications, there are several potential risks associated with prolonged delay when issues have been identified.

Can Impacted Wisdom Teeth Cause Recurrent Infections?

Pericoronitis â€” infection of the gum tissue around a partially erupted wisdom tooth â€” is one of the most common complications. When this occurs once, it can often be managed with cleaning and, where appropriate, antibiotics. However, if the underlying cause (the impacted tooth) remains, the infection may recur.

Repeated episodes of pericoronitis can be uncomfortable and disruptive, and in some cases, the infections may become progressively more difficult to manage with conservative measures alone.

Could Neighbouring Teeth Be Affected?

An impacted wisdom tooth that is pressing against the adjacent second molar may, over time, contribute to decay in that neighbouring tooth. The pressure and the difficulty of cleaning between a partially erupted wisdom tooth and its neighbour can create conditions where decay is more likely to develop.

In some cases, this can result in the need for treatment on a tooth that was previously healthy â€” which is why monitoring the relationship between an impacted wisdom tooth and its neighbours is clinically important.

Is There a Risk of Bone or Gum Damage?

In certain situations, an impacted wisdom tooth may contribute to localised bone loss or gum pocket formation around neighbouring teeth. This can occur gradually over time and may not cause obvious symptoms in the early stages.

Additionally, though uncommon, cysts or other pathology can occasionally develop around impacted teeth that remain in the jaw for extended periods. This is another reason why periodic monitoring through dental examinations is important, even when symptoms are absent.

A balanced perspective: The risks outlined above are potential complications â€” they do not affect every patient with impacted wisdom teeth. The decision to remove a wisdom tooth should always be based on individual clinical findings, not on generalised assumptions. Your dentist can help you understand your specific level of risk.

When Early Removal May Be Recommended

There are certain clinical scenarios where your dentist may recommend removal of an impacted wisdom tooth sooner rather than later. This recommendation is based on professional assessment and aims to reduce the risk of complications developing or worsening. Situations where earlier intervention may be considered include:

Repeated Pain or Infection

If you have experienced more than one episode of pain or infection (pericoronitis) associated with an impacted wisdom tooth, this pattern may suggest that conservative management alone is unlikely to resolve the issue long-term. In such cases, removal may be discussed as a more definitive approach.

Difficulty Maintaining Oral Hygiene

Partially erupted wisdom teeth can be very difficult to clean effectively, even with careful brushing and flossing. If the position of the tooth makes it consistently challenging to keep the area free from plaque and food debris, the ongoing risk of decay and gum disease in that area may support a recommendation for removal.

Evidence of Damage to Adjacent Teeth

If X-rays or clinical examination reveal that an impacted wisdom tooth is contributing to decay, root resorption, or bone loss affecting the neighbouring second molar, removal of the wisdom tooth may be recommended to protect the health of the adjacent tooth.

Clinical Judgement Based on Examination and Imaging

In some cases, the position and angulation of an impacted wisdom tooth may suggest a higher likelihood of future problems, even if symptoms have not yet developed. Your dentist will use clinical examination and X-rays to assess factors such as the degree of impaction, proximity to nerves and other structures, and the overall health of the surrounding tissues.

It's important to understand that a recommendation for removal is not a demand â€” it's a professional opinion intended to support your decision-making. You should feel comfortable asking questions about why removal is being suggested and what the alternatives might be.

When Monitoring May Be Appropriate

Not every impacted wisdom tooth requires removal. In many cases, a more conservative approach â€” ongoing monitoring through regular dental appointments â€” may be the most appropriate course of action.

When Might Monitoring Be Recommended?

Your dentist may suggest monitoring rather than removal when:

  • The impacted wisdom tooth is causing no symptoms â€” no pain, no swelling, and no signs of infection
  • X-rays show no evidence of decay, cyst formation, or damage to neighbouring teeth
  • The tooth is deeply impacted and unlikely to cause problems in its current position
  • The area can be kept clean with good oral hygiene practices
  • There are medical or clinical factors that mean the risks of removal may outweigh the benefits at this time

The Role of Regular Dental Check-Ups

If monitoring is the chosen approach, regular dental examination appointments become particularly important. These allow your dentist to track any changes over time â€” such as shifts in the tooth's position, early signs of decay, or the development of gum pockets â€” and to adjust the management plan if needed.

Periodic X-rays may also be recommended to assess changes that aren't visible during a clinical examination alone.

Monitoring Is an Active Decision, Not a Passive One

Choosing to monitor a wisdom tooth is a valid clinical decision â€” but it's not the same as ignoring it. Active monitoring means attending regular appointments, maintaining good oral hygiene around the area, and being aware of any changes in symptoms that should prompt a review. If the situation changes, the recommendation may change too.

Wisdom Tooth Pain and Dental Emergencies

While many wisdom tooth issues develop gradually and can be managed through planned appointments, there are situations where symptoms may become severe enough to warrant urgent attention.

When Does Wisdom Tooth Pain Become Urgent?

Mild, intermittent discomfort around a wisdom tooth can often wait for a routine dental appointment. However, certain symptoms may indicate a more serious issue that requires prompt assessment:

  • Severe or worsening pain that is not adequately controlled by over-the-counter pain relief
  • Significant facial swelling that is spreading or increasing
  • Difficulty opening your mouth (trismus), which may suggest spreading infection
  • Fever or feeling generally unwell, which may indicate that an infection is affecting your wider health
  • Difficulty swallowing or swelling that is affecting your ability to breathe normally
  • Persistent bleeding from the gum around a wisdom tooth that doesn't stop with gentle pressure

What Should You Do If Symptoms Become Severe?

If you experience any of the symptoms listed above, it's important to seek professional advice promptly. Contact your dental practice in the first instance â€” many practices offer same-day or next-day emergency appointments for patients experiencing acute symptoms.

If your usual dental practice is closed, seeking care from an emergency dentist in London is advisable. Dental infections can occasionally progress and may require clinical intervention to manage safely.

Seek immediate medical attention if you experience difficulty breathing, inability to swallow, or rapidly spreading swelling in the face, neck, or throat. In these rare but serious situations, attend A&E or call 999.

Managing Discomfort While Awaiting an Appointment

If you are waiting for a dental appointment and experiencing discomfort, the following may help in the interim:

  • Over-the-counter pain relief such as paracetamol or ibuprofen, taken as directed on the packaging
  • Rinsing gently with warm salt water (half a teaspoon of salt dissolved in a glass of warm water)
  • Avoiding very hot, very cold, or hard foods on the affected side
  • Keeping the area as clean as possible with gentle brushing

These measures may help manage symptoms temporarily, but they do not treat the underlying cause. Professional assessment remains important.

What to Expect from Wisdom Tooth Removal

If removal is recommended and you decide to proceed, understanding what the process involves can help you feel more prepared and confident about your decision.

Initial Assessment and Diagnosis

Before any removal takes place, your dentist will carry out a thorough assessment. This typically includes:

  • A clinical examination of the wisdom tooth and surrounding area
  • X-rays (and in some cases, a CT scan) to assess the tooth's position, the shape of its roots, and its relationship to nearby structures such as nerves
  • A discussion of your medical history and any medications you are taking
  • An explanation of the procedure, including potential risks and benefits specific to your case

This assessment ensures that removal is the right approach for you and allows your dentist to plan the procedure carefully.

How Is an Impacted Wisdom Tooth Removed?

The complexity of the removal depends on the type and degree of impaction. In general terms:

  • A local anaesthetic is administered to numb the area around the tooth
  • If the tooth is partially or fully covered by gum tissue, a small incision may be made to access it
  • In some cases, a small amount of bone surrounding the tooth may need to be removed
  • The tooth may be sectioned (divided into smaller pieces) to allow easier removal with less disruption to surrounding tissue
  • Once the tooth is removed, the area is cleaned and stitches may be placed to support healing

The duration of the procedure varies depending on the complexity of the case. Your dentist will explain what to expect in your specific situation during the assessment appointment.

General Recovery Expectations

Recovery following wisdom tooth removal varies between individuals and depends on factors such as the complexity of the extraction, your general health, and how well you follow aftercare instructions. In general terms:

  • Some swelling, discomfort, and limited jaw opening is common in the first few days
  • Over-the-counter pain relief is usually sufficient to manage post-operative discomfort
  • Soft foods are typically recommended for the first few days
  • Most people are able to return to normal activities within a few days, though this varies
  • Your dentist will provide specific aftercare instructions tailored to your procedure

Good to know: Your dentist will provide clear aftercare guidance and will be available to answer any questions or concerns that arise during your recovery. A follow-up appointment may be arranged to check that healing is progressing well.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do all impacted wisdom teeth need to be removed?

No. Many impacted wisdom teeth can be safely monitored if they are not causing symptoms, infection, or damage to surrounding teeth. The decision to remove a wisdom tooth is based on individual clinical assessment, not on a blanket recommendation. Your dentist will advise you based on your specific situation.

How do I know if my wisdom tooth is impacted?

Impacted wisdom teeth are typically identified through dental X-rays, often during a routine check-up. You may notice symptoms such as pain or swelling at the back of your mouth, but some impacted teeth cause no symptoms at all. A professional assessment is the most reliable way to determine whether your wisdom teeth are impacted.

What is pericoronitis?

Pericoronitis is an infection of the gum tissue that partially covers an erupting or impacted tooth, most commonly a wisdom tooth. It can cause pain, swelling, difficulty chewing, and sometimes a bad taste in the mouth. It may resolve with cleaning and antibiotics, but recurrent episodes may indicate that removal of the affected tooth should be considered.

Is wisdom tooth removal painful?

Local anaesthetic is used during the procedure to numb the area, so you should not feel pain during the removal itself. Some discomfort, swelling, and jaw stiffness are common after the procedure and typically improve over the following days. Your dentist will provide guidance on managing post-operative discomfort.

How long does it take to recover from wisdom tooth removal?

Recovery varies between individuals and depends on the complexity of the extraction. Many people feel significantly better within a few days, though complete healing of the extraction site may take several weeks. Your dentist will provide aftercare instructions and will be available if you have any concerns during recovery.

Can I wait and see if my wisdom tooth causes problems before deciding?

In many cases, yes â€” this is what monitoring involves. However, it's important that this is an active, informed decision made in consultation with your dentist, rather than simply avoiding assessment. Regular check-ups allow any changes to be identified early and managed appropriately.

When should I seek emergency care for wisdom tooth pain?

You should seek urgent dental care if you experience severe pain that is not controlled by over-the-counter medication, significant or spreading facial swelling, difficulty opening your mouth or swallowing, fever, or persistent bleeding. These symptoms may indicate a more serious infection that requires prompt professional assessment.

Should I have all four wisdom teeth removed at once?

This depends on your individual circumstances. Some patients may benefit from having multiple wisdom teeth removed in a single appointment, while others may prefer a staged approach. Your dentist will discuss the options with you and recommend the approach that best suits your clinical needs and personal preferences.
