Introduction

Breaking a tooth — whether from biting down on something hard, a sports injury, or gradual wear — is one of the most common reasons adults seek dental advice. Many people turn to an internet search first, hoping to understand whether their tooth can be saved, what the options are, and how urgently they need to act.

One of the most frequent questions that arises in this situation is: can you put a crown on a broken tooth? The short answer is that a dental crown is often a well-established restorative option for broken teeth — but suitability depends on several clinical factors that only a dentist can properly evaluate.

This article explains what dental crowns are, how they work, when they may be appropriate for a broken tooth, and what other treatment options might be considered. Understanding your options can help you feel more informed when speaking to your dentist, and may help you recognise when professional attention is needed sooner rather than later.

Can You Put a Crown on a Broken Tooth?

Yes, a dental crown can often be placed on a broken tooth to restore its shape, strength, and function. A crown covers the entire visible tooth surface and is commonly used when a tooth is too damaged for a filling alone. Suitability depends on the extent of the break and the condition of the remaining tooth structure, and should always be assessed by a dentist.

What Is a Dental Crown and How Does It Work?

A dental crown is a tooth-shaped cap that fits over the entire visible portion of a natural tooth — from the gum line upwards. It is designed to restore a tooth's original shape, size, and function, and can also improve the appearance of the tooth in question.

Crowns are typically made from a range of materials, including:

  • Porcelain or ceramic – often chosen for front teeth due to their natural tooth-like appearance
  • Porcelain-fused-to-metal (PFM) – combining strength with a natural look
  • Zirconia – highly durable and increasingly popular for both front and back teeth
  • Metal alloys – exceptionally strong, sometimes used for back teeth where aesthetics are less of a priority

The crown is cemented firmly onto the prepared tooth, effectively encasing it and protecting it from further damage. For a crown to be placed successfully, there must be enough healthy tooth structure remaining to support it — this is one of the key considerations a dentist will assess before recommending this option.

Crowns are a well-established restorative treatment in dentistry and, when properly placed and cared for, can last many years. Individual outcomes, however, will vary depending on oral hygiene, habits, and the specific condition of the tooth.

When Might a Crown Be Considered for a Broken Tooth?

Not every broken tooth will need a crown — the appropriate treatment depends on the type, size, and location of the break, as well as the overall health of the tooth and surrounding structures. A dentist will assess these factors during a clinical examination, which may include dental X-rays.

A crown may be considered when:

  • A significant portion of the tooth has broken away, leaving insufficient structure for a filling to hold reliably
  • The tooth has a large existing filling that has weakened the remaining tooth walls
  • The tooth has undergone root canal treatment, which can leave it more brittle and susceptible to fracture
  • The break extends below the gum line but not into the root, as this may still allow for crown placement in some cases
  • The tooth is cracked in a way that requires protection from biting forces to prevent the crack from deepening

It is important to note that if the root of the tooth is severely damaged, fractured vertically, or the surrounding bone has been significantly compromised, a crown may not be a viable option. In such cases, your dentist will discuss alternative treatments, which may include extraction and tooth replacement options.

If you have broken a tooth, seeking prompt dental assessment can help preserve more of the natural tooth structure and expand the range of treatment options available to you.

Understanding Tooth Anatomy: Why Structure Matters

To understand why the extent of a break is so important, it helps to have a basic understanding of tooth structure.

A tooth is made up of several layers:

  • Enamel – the hard outer shell of the tooth, the strongest substance in the human body. It protects the inner layers from damage and bacterial entry.
  • Dentine – the layer beneath the enamel, slightly softer and containing microscopic tubules that connect to the nerve. When exposed, dentine can cause sensitivity to temperature or pressure.
  • Pulp – the innermost layer containing nerves and blood vessels. If a break reaches the pulp, it can cause pain or lead to infection.
  • Root – the portion of the tooth anchored within the jawbone, surrounded by a layer called cementum and supported by the periodontal ligament.

When a tooth breaks, the treatment pathway depends on which layers are involved. A minor chip affecting only the enamel may require little more than smoothing or a small composite restoration. A deeper break involving the dentine may cause sensitivity and require more comprehensive treatment. A fracture reaching the pulp may necessitate root canal treatment before a crown can be placed.

This is why clinical examination — including X-rays to assess root and bone health — is essential before any treatment decision is made.

What Does the Crown Placement Process Typically Involve?

Understanding what to expect from the crown placement process may help reduce any anxiety you have about the procedure. While specific steps can vary depending on the clinical situation, a typical process for crowning a broken tooth may include:

1. Initial Assessment

Your dentist will examine the broken tooth, assess the damage, and take X-rays to evaluate the root and supporting bone. If the tooth has been damaged to the pulp, root canal treatment may be required before proceeding.

2. Tooth Preparation

The tooth is shaped to create a stable foundation for the crown. This involves removing a small amount of tooth structure from the outer surfaces so the crown sits flush with the surrounding teeth.

3. Impressions or Digital Scanning

Accurate impressions or digital scans of the prepared tooth are taken and used to fabricate the custom crown in a dental laboratory. Increasingly, some clinics use CAD/CAM technology to produce crowns on-site in a single visit.

4. Temporary Crown

While the permanent crown is being made, a temporary crown is usually fitted to protect the prepared tooth.

5. Fitting the Permanent Crown

Once ready, the permanent crown is carefully checked for fit, bite, and appearance before being cemented into place.

Are There Alternative Treatments for a Broken Tooth?

Depending on the nature and severity of the break, your dentist may discuss a range of treatment options. A crown is one of several restorative approaches, and the most appropriate choice will be determined following clinical assessment.

Other treatments that may be considered include:

  • Dental bonding (composite resin) – for minor chips or small breaks, a tooth-coloured resin material can be applied to restore the shape of the tooth. This is a less invasive option but may not be suitable for large breaks. For a broader cosmetic scope, see our guide on how many teeth can have composite bonding.
  • Inlay or onlay – a custom-made restoration that covers part of the tooth. An onlay extends over one or more cusps and may be suitable where damage is too extensive for a filling but a full crown is not yet necessary.
  • Veneer – primarily used for cosmetic improvement of front teeth; a veneer covers only the front surface and is generally not appropriate for significantly broken teeth.
  • Tooth extraction – where a tooth is too severely damaged to be restored, extraction may be the only viable option. Your dentist will then discuss tooth replacement solutions.
  • Root canal treatment followed by a crown – if the pulp is involved, this two-stage approach may be recommended to save the tooth.

Your dentist will explain the advantages and limitations of each option in the context of your individual circumstances. It is worth having an open conversation about your goals, concerns, and any practical considerations such as cost or treatment timescales. When Should You Seek Professional Dental Assessment?

If you have broken a tooth, it is generally advisable to seek dental advice as soon as you are able to. Even if you are not in significant pain, a broken tooth can leave the inner layers exposed to bacteria and physical forces that may cause the damage to worsen over time.

You should consider contacting your dentist promptly if you experience any of the following:

  • Persistent or worsening pain following a break
  • Sensitivity to hot or cold temperatures that lingers after the stimulus is removed
  • Swelling in the gum, jaw, or face near the affected tooth
  • A visible crack or fracture in the tooth
  • Bleeding or discomfort in the gum around the broken tooth
  • Difficulty biting or chewing on that side of the mouth
  • A feeling of pressure, throbbing, or a bad taste, which may suggest infection

These are not necessarily indicators of a serious problem, but they do suggest that a clinical assessment would be beneficial. A dentist can examine the tooth properly, take any necessary X-rays, and advise on the most appropriate course of action.

If you are experiencing symptoms that may suggest dental infection — such as significant swelling, fever, or difficulty swallowing or breathing — you should seek urgent dental or medical care.

Prevention and Oral Health: Protecting Your Teeth from Breaking

While not all dental fractures can be prevented, there are several practical steps you can take to reduce the risk of breaking a tooth and to support your overall oral health.

Watch what you bite on: Avoid using your teeth to open packaging, crack nuts, or chew on ice, pens, or other hard objects. These habits can cause fractures in even healthy teeth.

Wear a mouthguard during contact sports: A well-fitted mouthguard can significantly reduce the risk of dental trauma during physical activity. Your dentist can provide a custom-made mouthguard for better protection and comfort.

Address teeth grinding (bruxism): Grinding or clenching teeth — particularly at night — places considerable stress on tooth structure and can increase the risk of cracks and fractures over time. If you suspect you grind your teeth, speak to your dentist, as a custom-made night guard may help.

Maintain regular dental check-ups: Routine examinations allow your dentist to identify early signs of wear, cracks, or decay before they develop into more significant problems. Early detection generally means simpler, less invasive treatment.

Keep your enamel strong: A balanced diet low in acidic and sugary foods and drinks, combined with fluoride toothpaste twice daily, supports enamel health and reduces vulnerability to damage.

Key Points to Remember

  • Not all broken teeth require a crown — your dentist will assess which treatment is most appropriate based on a clinical examination.
  • Tooth anatomy plays an important role: breaks involving the pulp may require root canal treatment before a crown can be placed.
  • If a tooth is too severely damaged, a crown may not be possible, and alternative options such as extraction and tooth replacement will be discussed.
  • Seeking dental advice promptly after breaking a tooth can help preserve more treatment options.
  • Protective habits — such as wearing a mouthguard and attending regular check-ups — can help reduce the risk of future dental fractures.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a severely broken tooth always be saved with a crown?

Not necessarily. Whether a crown can be placed on a broken tooth depends on how much healthy tooth structure remains and the condition of the root and surrounding bone. If the root is severely fractured or the supporting structures are significantly compromised, a crown may not be viable. Your dentist will assess the tooth thoroughly, including X-rays, before advising on whether a crown or an alternative treatment is the most suitable option for your situation.

Does getting a crown on a broken tooth hurt?

The preparation and placement of a dental crown is typically carried out under local anaesthetic, which numbs the area so you should not feel pain during the procedure. Some patients experience mild sensitivity or discomfort in the tooth and surrounding gum for a few days after treatment, which usually settles. If you experience significant or prolonged pain following crown placement, it is advisable to contact your dentist so they can check that everything has settled as expected.

How long does a dental crown last on a broken tooth?

With good oral hygiene and regular dental check-ups, a dental crown can last many years — often ten years or more. However, individual outcomes vary and depend on factors such as the material used, oral hygiene habits, diet, whether you grind your teeth, and the clinical conditions of the tooth at the time of placement. Your dentist will be able to advise you on how to care for your crown and what to expect in terms of longevity based on your specific circumstances.

What happens if I leave a broken tooth untreated?

Leaving a broken tooth without professional assessment and treatment can allow bacteria to enter the exposed inner layers of the tooth, potentially leading to decay, infection, or abscess. The break may also worsen over time, particularly under normal biting forces, reducing the likelihood of being able to save the tooth. Some patients experience increased sensitivity or pain as the condition progresses. Whilst not every broken tooth requires urgent treatment, prompt dental assessment is generally recommended to understand the extent of the damage and the options available.

Can a crown be placed on a tooth that has had a root canal?

Yes, a dental crown is often recommended following root canal treatment. Root canal treatment involves removing the pulp tissue from inside the tooth, which can leave the tooth more brittle and vulnerable to fracture. Placing a crown over the treated tooth helps to protect it and restore its function. The suitability of crowning a root-treated tooth will still depend on the amount of remaining natural tooth structure and the overall condition of the tooth and root.

Will my dental insurance or plan cover a crown on a broken tooth?

Coverage for dental crowns varies considerably depending on the type of dental plan or insurance policy you hold. Some plans include restorative treatments as part of their coverage; others may require additional payment or have limits on the materials covered. It is advisable to check the terms of your specific plan before treatment. Your dental practice should also be able to provide a clear treatment plan and associated costs prior to proceeding, allowing you to make an informed decision.
