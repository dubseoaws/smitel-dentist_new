Introduction

If you have noticed small dark gaps forming at the base of your teeth — particularly near the gumline — you are not alone. These spaces, commonly referred to as black triangles between teeth, are a surprisingly common cosmetic and clinical concern that many adults in the UK notice and search about online.

Black triangles (clinically known as open gingival embrasures) can make teeth appear uneven, ageing, or spaced, even when the teeth themselves are otherwise healthy. As a result, many people begin researching cosmetic dental options — including veneers — to understand whether these gaps can be improved aesthetically.

This article explains what black triangles are, why they develop, and how dental veneers may be able to influence their appearance. It also covers the clinical factors that affect suitability, the limitations of cosmetic treatment, and when seeking a professional dental assessment is the most appropriate step.

Understanding the options available to you is the first step towards making an informed decision about your smile.

Can Veneers Help with Black Triangles Between Teeth?

Yes, dental veneers can help reduce the visible appearance of black triangles between teeth in certain cases. By slightly widening the visible surface of adjacent teeth, veneers may minimise the perceived gap. However, suitability depends entirely on individual clinical factors and must be assessed by a qualified dental professional during examination.

What Are Black Triangles Between Teeth?

Black triangles between teeth are the small, dark, triangular spaces that appear at the point where two teeth meet near the gumline. They form when the gum tissue — known as the interdental papilla — no longer fills the space between adjacent teeth completely.

From a distance, these gaps can appear as dark, shadow-like voids, which can make a smile look uneven or give the impression that teeth are separated or shifting.

It is worth noting that black triangles are not always purely a cosmetic concern. In some instances, they can indicate changes in gum tissue health or bone support around the teeth. This is why any noticeable change in the appearance of your gumline is worth discussing with a dental professional, even if the spaces appear small or cause no discomfort.

The size and position of black triangles vary significantly between individuals. Some people notice them only in certain areas — often between the upper front teeth — whilst others may observe them across several teeth. Understanding what has caused them in your specific case is an important part of identifying appropriate management options.

Common Causes of Black Triangles Between Teeth

Black triangles can develop for a variety of reasons. In many cases, multiple contributing factors are present at the same time.

Gum recession or gum disease: A frequent underlying cause. When gum tissue pulls away from the tooth, the natural contour of the papilla is reduced, revealing a gap. Periodontal disease can accelerate this process significantly.

Natural tooth shape: Teeth that are more triangular in shape (wider at the biting edge and narrower at the base) naturally leave less space for gum tissue to fill the contact point, making gaps more visible.

Bone loss: The gum tissue follows the underlying bone level. If bone has reduced — through gum disease, ageing, or other factors — the gum often recedes with it.

Orthodontic treatment: Moving teeth with braces or aligners can occasionally alter the contact points between teeth, sometimes revealing existing gaps that were previously hidden.

Ageing: As we age, gum tissue naturally thins and loses some of its volume, making black triangles more likely to appear.

Previous dental treatment: Poorly contoured restorations or certain hygiene techniques may contribute to changes in gum tissue over time.

Understanding the cause is essential before any cosmetic treatment is considered, as some underlying causes — such as active gum disease — should be managed first.

How Veneers May Affect the Appearance of Black Triangles

Dental veneers are thin, custom-made shells — typically made from porcelain or composite resin — that are bonded to the front surface of the teeth. They are primarily used to improve the colour, shape, and overall appearance of teeth.

In the context of black triangles between teeth, veneers may help in a few specific ways:

Widening the tooth profile: By extending the contact point of adjacent teeth slightly downward or outward at the design stage, a skilled clinician and dental technician can create a veneer shape that reduces the perceived size of the gap. This is sometimes described as adding embrasure fill.

Improving tooth symmetry: Veneers designed to optimise tooth proportions can make the overall smile appear more harmonious, drawing less visual attention to remaining gaps.

Masking minor spacing: For small black triangles that are primarily cosmetic in nature and not associated with significant gum tissue loss, veneers may offer a visible improvement.

However, there are important limitations to understand. Veneers sit on the front surface of the tooth — they do not replace or regenerate lost gum tissue. If a black triangle is particularly deep or wide due to significant gum recession, veneers alone are unlikely to close the gap entirely. In such cases, alternative or combined approaches may be more appropriate.

The Clinical Science Behind Black Triangles

To understand why black triangles develop and why treatment outcomes vary, it helps to understand a little about the anatomy of the gum tissue around teeth.

Between any two adjacent teeth, there is a small pyramid-shaped piece of gum tissue called the interdental papilla. This papilla sits within what is called the embrasure space — the V-shaped gap between teeth at the gumline.

Healthy papillae fill this space completely, giving the appearance of a smooth, even gumline. The height of the papilla depends on two critical factors: the level of the underlying alveolar bone (the bone that supports the teeth) and the position of the contact point where the two teeth touch each other.

Research has shown that when the distance from the contact point between two teeth to the underlying bone crest exceeds approximately 5 millimetres, the papilla often fails to fill the space entirely — resulting in a visible black triangle. As bone level reduces due to gum disease or ageing, this distance increases, making complete papilla fill progressively less likely.

This is why cosmetic solutions such as veneers can sometimes reduce the visual appearance of a black triangle by lowering the contact point — but cannot recreate the biological gum tissue itself. For patients where gum tissue loss is significant, a clinician with advanced periodontal training may need to be involved in the treatment planning discussion.

Other Treatment Options for Black Triangles

Veneers are not the only option available for managing the appearance of black triangles. A dental professional may discuss several approaches depending on your clinical situation:

Composite bonding: A tooth-coloured composite resin material can be carefully shaped and applied to the sides of adjacent teeth to fill in or reduce the appearance of the gap. This is often a conservative, reversible option suitable for smaller gaps, and you can read more in our related article on how composite bonding can change sensations between front teeth.

Dental crowns: In cases where the existing teeth require more extensive restoration, crowns may be considered as part of an overall treatment plan.

Periodontal treatment: If active gum disease is contributing to the black triangles, addressing the underlying gum health through professional cleaning and periodontal therapy is often the essential first step before any cosmetic treatment is discussed.

Hyaluronic acid injections: This is a relatively newer approach in which hyaluronic acid is carefully injected into the papilla area to temporarily restore some volume to the gum tissue. This is a clinician-delivered procedure with limited long-term evidence, and its suitability varies significantly between patients.

Orthodontics: In some cases, carefully planned tooth movement can bring adjacent teeth closer together, effectively reducing the embrasure space and making the black triangle less visible.

The most appropriate option will depend on the size and cause of the black triangle, the health of the surrounding gum and bone, and your individual clinical circumstances. Each treatment has its own benefits, limitations, and maintenance requirements.

Prevention and Maintaining Good Oral Health

Whilst not all black triangles can be prevented — particularly those resulting from natural tooth shape or ageing — there are several steps that support long-term gum health and may reduce the risk of gum recession contributing to their development.

Maintain a thorough but gentle oral hygiene routine. Brushing too vigorously with a hard-bristled toothbrush can gradually wear away gum tissue over time. Using a soft-bristled brush and gentle circular movements is generally recommended.

Floss or use interdental brushes daily. Keeping the spaces between teeth clean reduces the risk of plaque accumulation, which is a significant contributing factor to gum disease and subsequent gum recession.

Attend regular dental check-ups and hygiene appointments. Professional cleaning removes tartar build-up that cannot be addressed through home care alone, helping to preserve gum health over time.

Address gum disease early. If you notice bleeding gums, swelling, or tenderness, seeking a professional assessment promptly is advisable. Gum disease that is detected and treated early is more likely to respond well to management.

Avoid smoking. Smoking significantly impairs gum health and is a well-established risk factor for periodontal disease and gum recession.

When a Professional Dental Assessment May Be Appropriate

Whilst black triangles are not always a sign of a serious dental problem, there are circumstances where seeking professional advice sooner rather than later is sensible.

You notice the gaps are getting larger or more numerous. This may suggest an ongoing process such as gum recession or bone loss that would benefit from assessment.

Your gums bleed regularly when brushing or flossing. Bleeding gums are a common early indicator of gum disease. It is worth mentioning this at your next dental appointment, even if there is no pain.

You experience sensitivity around the gaps. Exposed tooth root surfaces near receded gum tissue can sometimes cause sensitivity to temperature or touch.

You are considering cosmetic treatment. Before committing to veneers or any cosmetic procedure, a thorough clinical assessment is essential to determine whether your gum health, tooth structure, and bite are suitable for the planned treatment.

You are unsure of the cause. If you are not certain why black triangles have appeared or seem to be worsening, a dental professional can carry out an examination, take any necessary radiographs, and help you understand the underlying reason.

Seeking an assessment early allows for a wider range of management options and, where relevant, supports better long-term outcomes.

Key Points to Remember

  • Black triangles between teeth are spaces that form when gum tissue no longer fills the gap between adjacent teeth at the gumline.
  • They can be caused by gum recession, gum disease, natural tooth shape, bone loss, ageing, or previous dental treatment.
  • Dental veneers may help reduce the visual appearance of black triangles by altering the tooth profile and lowering the contact point between teeth, but results vary depending on individual clinical factors.
  • Veneers cannot replace lost gum tissue; suitability must always be assessed during a clinical examination.
  • Other options such as composite bonding, periodontal treatment, or hyaluronic acid procedures may also be appropriate in certain cases.
  • Maintaining good oral hygiene, attending regular dental appointments, and addressing gum disease early are the most effective steps for preserving gum health over time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do black triangles between teeth always need treatment?

Not necessarily. Small black triangles that are stable, not associated with active gum disease, and not causing concern may simply require monitoring during regular dental check-ups. However, if they are growing in size, associated with bleeding or sensitivity, or causing cosmetic distress, it is worth discussing management options with a dental professional. Treatment decisions should always be guided by a clinical assessment rather than general information alone.

Can veneers completely close black triangles?

Veneers can reduce the visible appearance of black triangles in some cases, but they are unlikely to close them entirely — particularly where gum tissue loss is significant. By carefully designing the shape and contact point of the veneers, a skilled dentist may be able to minimise the perceived gap. However, the degree of improvement will depend on the size of the triangle, the amount of gum tissue present, and the overall anatomy of the surrounding teeth. This should be discussed openly during a consultation.

Are black triangles a sign of gum disease?

Black triangles can be associated with gum disease, but they are not always caused by it. Natural tooth shape, ageing, and certain dental treatments can also result in these gaps. However, because gum disease is a common underlying factor, it is important to have your gum health assessed if you notice black triangles developing or changing. Active gum disease should generally be treated before any cosmetic intervention is considered.

How long do veneers last if used to improve the appearance of black triangles?

Porcelain veneers typically last between 10 and 15 years with good care, though individual outcomes vary. Composite veneers may require more frequent maintenance or replacement. Veneer longevity depends on factors such as oral hygiene, biting habits, and how well the underlying teeth and gums remain healthy over time. Regular dental check-ups are important for monitoring veneer condition and overall oral health.

Is composite bonding a better option than veneers for black triangles?

For smaller black triangles, composite bonding can be a conservative and cost-effective option. It involves less or no removal of tooth structure and is generally reversible. For more extensive cosmetic changes or where additional improvements to tooth colour or shape are desired, porcelain veneers may offer more durable and aesthetically consistent results. The most appropriate option for your individual situation can only be determined after a thorough dental examination.

Can black triangles return after veneer treatment?

Yes, it is possible. If the underlying cause of the black triangle — such as gum recession or bone loss — continues to progress, the gap may reappear or widen even after veneer placement. This highlights the importance of addressing any active gum disease before cosmetic treatment and maintaining good oral hygiene and regular dental visits afterwards. A dental professional can advise on the likelihood of this based on your individual clinical situation.

Conclusion

Black triangles between teeth are a common concern that affects many adults, and it is entirely understandable to seek information about how they might be managed or improved. Dental veneers can play a role in reducing the visible appearance of black triangles between teeth in appropriate cases — primarily by altering the shape and contact point of adjacent teeth to minimise the perceived gap.

However, veneers are not a universal solution. Their suitability depends on the size of the black triangle, the health of the surrounding gum and bone tissue, and a range of individual clinical factors. In some cases, other treatments — such as composite bonding, periodontal therapy, or a combination of approaches — may be more appropriate.

The most important step for anyone concerned about black triangles is to seek professional dental advice. A qualified dentist can assess your gum health, identify any underlying causes, and discuss the options that may be suitable for your specific circumstances.

Dental symptoms and treatment options should always be assessed individually during a clinical examination.
