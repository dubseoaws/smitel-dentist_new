Introduction

Many patients are puzzled when a dental crown that causes no discomfort during waking hours suddenly becomes sensitive, achy, or uncomfortable at night. It is one of those dental concerns that feels confusing precisely because of its inconsistency — if something were truly wrong, surely it would hurt all the time?

Crown sensitivity at night is more common than you might expect, and the good news is that it is often explainable. There are several well-understood reasons why a crowned tooth might behave differently when you are lying down or asleep compared to when you are upright and active during the day.

This article explores the most likely causes of night-time crown sensitivity, explains the underlying dental science in plain language, and outlines when it may be worth speaking to a dental professional. Understanding why this happens can help you feel more informed and confident about your next steps — without unnecessary worry.

Why Is My Crown Sensitive at Night?

Crown sensitivity at night is often linked to teeth grinding (bruxism), changes in blood pressure when lying flat, or low-level inflammation around the tooth root that becomes more noticeable at rest. During the day, distractions and upright posture can mask these sensations. If crown sensitivity at night persists, a dental assessment is advisable.

What Is a Dental Crown and Why Might It Cause Sensitivity?

A dental crown is a custom-made cap placed over a damaged, weakened, or heavily restored tooth to restore its shape, strength, and appearance. Crowns are typically made from porcelain, ceramic, metal alloy, or a combination of these materials.

Although a crowned tooth no longer has its original outer enamel, the underlying tooth structure — including the dentine and, in many cases, a living pulp — remains present. This is an important point. Many patients assume that because a tooth has been crowned, it is effectively sealed off from sensation. In reality, the nerve tissue inside the tooth can still respond to pressure, temperature, inflammation, or infection.

A crown that was placed correctly and is functioning well should not ordinarily cause significant discomfort. However, certain changes — either to the crown itself, the supporting tooth, or surrounding tissues — can cause sensitivity to develop over time. When this sensitivity appears primarily at night, it often points to a specific set of causes rather than a general crown failure.

Common Reasons Crown Sensitivity Occurs at Night

1. Teeth Grinding and Clenching (Bruxism)

One of the most frequent causes of night-time dental discomfort — including sensitivity around a crown — is bruxism, the unconscious grinding or clenching of teeth during sleep. Many people are entirely unaware they grind their teeth until a partner mentions it or a dentist identifies wear patterns.

When you grind your teeth at night, significant lateral and compressive forces are applied to your teeth and crowns repeatedly over several hours. This can cause:

  • Micro-stress in the crown material
  • Soreness in the ligament that holds the tooth in the jawbone
  • Inflammation of the pulp tissue inside the tooth
  • Generalised jaw and facial muscle aching upon waking

Because grinding only occurs at night, daytime comfort is preserved. The jaw muscles and periodontal tissues have the day to recover, which is why discomfort may fade by mid-morning and return the following night.

If bruxism is suspected, a dentist may recommend a custom-made night guard or occlusal splint to protect the crown and surrounding teeth from further wear.

2. Changes in Blood Pressure and Body Position

When you lie down to sleep, your body's blood pressure and circulatory dynamics shift. Blood flow to the head and jaw increases relative to when you are sitting or standing upright. In teeth with any underlying pulp inflammation — even mild, subclinical inflammation — this increased blood pressure within the confined pulp chamber can cause a throbbing or aching sensation.

Think of it like a minor headache that worsens when you bend over. The same principle applies: increased pressure in an inflamed space causes increased pain.

This is why patients sometimes describe their crown as feeling "fine all day" but experiencing a dull, pressure-like ache once they lie down in bed. The sensation is real, not imagined, and it often suggests that the nerve tissue beneath the crown warrants investigation.

3. Referred Pain and Sinus Pressure

Upper back teeth (upper molars and premolars) sit in close anatomical proximity to the maxillary sinuses — the air-filled cavities in your cheekbones. When sinuses become congested or mildly inflamed — whether from seasonal allergies, a common cold, or chronic sinus issues — the resulting pressure can mimic toothache.

This is known as referred dental pain, and it can be difficult to distinguish from genuine tooth sensitivity without a thorough clinical assessment. Sinus pressure tends to increase when lying down, which explains why upper crown discomfort might present more prominently at night.

If you have recently had a cold, hay fever, or nasal congestion alongside your crown sensitivity, sinus involvement is worth considering and discussing with your dentist.

4. The Crown Bite May Be Slightly Off

Occlusion — the way your upper and lower teeth meet — is a finely calibrated system. When a new crown is placed, the dentist carefully adjusts the bite to ensure it sits correctly within your overall bite pattern. However, bite adjustments made in the dental chair are carried out whilst you are upright and sometimes slightly numb from local anaesthesia, which can make precise assessment challenging.

Over time, or if the bite was not quite right from the start, a crown that receives slightly too much force during chewing or grinding can become sore. Because the tooth experiences the most cumulative force whilst sleeping (particularly if grinding is present), night-time discomfort is a common presentation.

A simple bite adjustment — a quick and straightforward procedure — is often all that is required to resolve this type of sensitivity. If that sounds familiar, this related guide on a crown feeling too high after adjustment explains what to check next.

5. Underlying Pulp Inflammation or Early Nerve Involvement

The dental pulp — the soft tissue inside your tooth containing nerves and blood vessels — can become inflamed for a number of reasons, including deep decay prior to crown placement, trauma, or a crack within the tooth. This condition is known as pulpitis.

In its early stages, pulpitis may cause only mild or intermittent sensitivity, often more noticeable at night due to the positional and circulatory factors described above. As the condition progresses, symptoms typically become more consistent and pronounced.

Pulpitis does not always mean a tooth is beyond saving, but it does require professional assessment to determine the most appropriate course of management. Where deeper pulp involvement is suspected, understanding when a crown is needed after root canal treatment can also be helpful.

The Dental Science Behind Night-Time Crown Sensitivity

To understand why night-time specifically tends to amplify crown-related discomfort, it helps to briefly consider the anatomy of a crowned tooth and how the nervous system processes pain during sleep.

Tooth anatomy under a crown: Even beneath a crown, the tooth retains its internal structure. Dentine — the layer beneath the enamel — contains thousands of microscopic tubules that lead toward the pulp. If the crown margin has any minor gap, or if dentine was exposed during preparation, stimuli can travel through these tubules and trigger the nerve.

The pulp chamber and pressure: The pulp sits within a rigid space — the pulp chamber and root canals. Any inflammation within this space increases pressure on the nerve fibres, causing pain. Lying flat increases intra-pulpal pressure, which explains the nocturnal pattern.

Sleep and pain perception: Research in pain science also suggests that pain thresholds can shift during sleep. The absence of daytime distractions and reduced cortisol levels at night mean the nervous system may process background dental discomfort more acutely during the hours of rest.

When a Professional Dental Assessment May Be Needed

Crown sensitivity at night is not always a sign of an emergency, but there are certain symptoms that make a dental evaluation advisable sooner rather than later. You should consider booking an appointment if you experience:

  • Persistent aching that does not resolve after a few days
  • Sensitivity to hot or cold that lingers for more than a few seconds after the stimulus is removed
  • Swelling around the crowned tooth or in the gum
  • A bad taste or odour that may suggest infection
  • Pain upon biting or chewing that is worsening
  • Difficulty sleeping due to the discomfort

These symptoms do not necessarily indicate something serious, but a qualified dentist can examine the tooth, take X-rays where appropriate, and determine whether any treatment is needed. Early assessment is always preferable to waiting until symptoms become more severe.

If you are concerned about ongoing dental discomfort, it is worth exploring dental check-up and examination options with your dental practice to discuss what is happening with your crown.

Preventative and Oral Health Advice

Whilst not all causes of crown sensitivity are preventable, there are several practical steps you can take to protect your crowns and maintain good oral health:

Manage bruxism proactively. If you know or suspect you grind your teeth at night, speak to your dentist about a custom occlusal splint. These devices help distribute biting forces more evenly and protect both natural teeth and crowns from excessive wear.

Maintain excellent oral hygiene around the crown. The junction between the crown margin and the gum is a susceptible area for plaque accumulation. Brushing twice daily with a fluoride toothpaste, flossing carefully around the crown, and using interdental brushes where recommended all help reduce gum inflammation and protect the supporting structures.

Attend regular dental check-ups. Crowns should be reviewed periodically to check the bite, margins, and surrounding gum tissue. Issues are far easier to address when identified early.

Be mindful of hard foods. Avoid habitually chewing very hard foods — such as ice, hard boiled sweets, or unpopped popcorn kernels — which can place stress on crowns and the teeth beneath them.

Address sinus health. If you are prone to sinus congestion and notice your upper crown discomfort correlates with sinus episodes, managing sinus health (in consultation with your GP) may also reduce dental discomfort.

Key Points to Remember

  • Crown sensitivity at night is a recognised dental concern with several explainable causes, including bruxism, positional blood pressure changes, bite discrepancies, and pulp inflammation.
  • The most common cause is teeth grinding or clenching during sleep, which applies repeated forces to crowns and surrounding structures.
  • Lying flat increases blood flow to the head, which can amplify any underlying pulp inflammation and cause a throbbing or aching sensation.
  • Sinus congestion can mimic or contribute to upper crown discomfort at night.
  • A bite that sits slightly too high on a crown can cause cumulative soreness, particularly if grinding is also present.
  • Persistent, worsening, or swelling-associated symptoms should always prompt a dental appointment. Early assessment is advisable.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

Is it normal for a crown to be sensitive at night?

Some mild sensitivity following a new crown placement is not unusual and often settles within a few weeks as the tooth and surrounding tissues adjust. However, sensitivity that develops some time after a crown was placed, or that worsens over time, is less typical and worth investigating. Night-time sensitivity specifically may point to bruxism or underlying pulp involvement. A dental professional can assess the crown to determine whether any intervention is needed.

Could teeth grinding be causing my crown to hurt at night?

Yes, bruxism — the clenching and grinding of teeth during sleep — is one of the most common reasons for night-time crown discomfort. Many people are unaware they grind their teeth. Signs can include jaw stiffness or headaches upon waking, as well as sensitivity or soreness in crowned teeth. Your dentist can look for wear patterns and discuss whether a custom night guard may help protect your teeth and crown from grinding forces.

Can a crowned tooth still have nerve pain?

Yes. Unless the tooth has had root canal treatment, the pulp tissue — containing nerve fibres — remains inside the tooth beneath the crown. This means the tooth can still experience sensitivity to temperature, pressure, or inflammation. Even if a root canal was carried out prior to the crown being placed, some patients report a residual deep ache, which may be related to the surrounding ligament or bone rather than the tooth nerve itself.

What is pulpitis and could it explain my symptoms?

Pulpitis refers to inflammation of the dental pulp — the soft inner tissue of a tooth. It can occur due to deep decay, trauma, or following extensive dental work. Symptoms often include sensitivity that is disproportionate to the stimulus, lingering discomfort after hot or cold exposure, and aching that worsens at night. Pulpitis can be reversible (where the pulp heals) or irreversible (where root canal treatment may be required). A clinical examination and X-rays help determine the appropriate management.

Should I be worried if my crown aches when I lie down?

A positional ache when lying down is not uncommon and may simply reflect increased blood pressure to the area amplifying mild inflammation. However, it is sensible not to dismiss it entirely, particularly if the ache is persistent, worsening, or accompanied by other symptoms such as swelling or sensitivity to temperature. Booking a dental appointment for an assessment will help identify or rule out any underlying issue and provide peace of mind.

How long should I wait before seeing a dentist about crown sensitivity?

If sensitivity is mild and has only just begun — particularly following recent crown placement — waiting one to two weeks to see whether it settles is reasonable. However, if sensitivity is worsening, is accompanied by swelling, a bad taste, or significant pain upon biting, it is worth seeking dental advice promptly rather than waiting. When in doubt, a brief consultation with your dental practice can help you decide on the most appropriate next steps.

Conclusion

Crown sensitivity at night can be unsettling, particularly when the tooth behaves perfectly well during waking hours. As this article has explored, there are several well-understood reasons why this pattern occurs — from the effects of bruxism and positional blood pressure changes to bite discrepancies, sinus influence, and early pulp inflammation. None of these causes should be a source of alarm, but all deserve considered attention.

Understanding that crown sensitivity at night has specific, identifiable explanations helps patients approach the issue calmly and constructively. In many cases, a relatively straightforward intervention — such as a bite adjustment or night guard — can make a significant difference. In others, more thorough investigation may be warranted.

If you have been experiencing night-time sensitivity around a crown, the most helpful step you can take is to discuss it with your dentist. They can carry out a full assessment, review the crown and surrounding tooth structures, and recommend the most appropriate course of action based on your individual clinical picture.
