Introduction

Discovering that a dental crown has come loose or fallen out entirely can be an unsettling experience. Whether you notice it whilst eating, feel unusual sensitivity on the affected tooth, or suddenly find your crown in your hand, it is a surprisingly common dental concern — and one that prompts many patients to search online for immediate guidance.

Dental crowns are designed to be long-lasting restorations, but over time, the cement that holds them in place can weaken, or the underlying tooth structure may change. Understanding what causes a crown to come loose and what your options are can help you feel more prepared and less anxious.

This article explains what recementation of a dental crown involves, when it may be possible, what factors a dentist will consider, and how to care for your tooth in the meantime. It also covers when professional dental assessment is important and what steps you can take to support the longevity of your restorations.

Can a Dental Crown Be Recemented?

Yes, in many cases a dental crown can be recemented onto the prepared tooth. A dentist will first assess the crown and underlying tooth to determine whether recementation is appropriate. If the crown is undamaged and the tooth structure remains sound, the dentist can clean and reattach the crown using dental cement. Suitability always depends on clinical assessment.

Why Do Dental Crowns Come Loose?

Dental crowns are intended to be durable, long-term restorations, but several factors can contribute to a crown becoming loose or detaching entirely. Understanding these causes can help patients recognise the signs early and seek timely professional advice.

Cement failure is one of the most frequent reasons. Dental cement, whilst strong, can degrade over time — particularly if exposed to repeated moisture, chewing forces, or acidity in the diet. As the cement weakens, the bond between the crown and the tooth gradually diminishes.

Decay beneath the crown is another important cause. If bacteria work their way under the crown margin, they can cause decay in the underlying tooth structure. As the tooth changes shape due to decay, the crown may no longer fit securely.

Wear and grinding (bruxism) places significant mechanical stress on crowns. Patients who clench or grind their teeth may find that their restorations loosen more rapidly than expected.

Natural tooth changes — including minor shifts in tooth position or changes to surrounding gum tissue — can also affect the fit of an existing crown over time.

In some cases, a crown may have been in place for many years and simply reached the end of its clinical lifespan. Most crowns have an expected longevity of around 10 to 15 years, though this varies considerably depending on material, placement, and oral health habits.

Can a Crown Always Be Recemented?

This is one of the most common questions patients ask when a crown becomes loose, and the honest answer is: it depends. Recementation is possible in many situations, but suitability must always be confirmed through a clinical examination.

When recementation may be appropriate:

  • The crown itself is structurally intact with no cracks or chips
  • The underlying tooth structure is healthy and sufficient to support the crown
  • The crown fits accurately onto the prepared tooth
  • There is no active decay beneath the crown margin

When recementation may not be suitable:

  • Significant decay has developed in the tooth beneath the crown
  • The crown has cracked, fractured, or no longer fits properly
  • The underlying tooth has broken or the root is compromised
  • The crown has been out of place for an extended period and the tooth has shifted

In cases where recementation is not clinically appropriate, your dentist will discuss alternative restorative options. These may include a replacement crown, or in some circumstances, a different treatment approach depending on the condition of the underlying tooth.

If assessment shows that the inner tooth structure is no longer healthy enough to support the crown, your dentist may also discuss whether root canal treatment is needed before a long-term restoration can be planned.

If you are unsure whether your crown can be recemented, the most important step is to arrange a dental assessment as soon as reasonably possible. You can learn more about dental crown treatment options at Smile Dentist to understand what options may be considered.

The Recementation Process: What to Expect at Your Appointment

Understanding what happens during a recementation appointment can help alleviate any anxiety you may feel about the procedure.

When you attend your dental appointment following a loose or dislodged crown, your dentist will begin with a thorough clinical assessment. This typically includes a visual examination of both the crown and the underlying tooth, and may involve dental X-rays to evaluate the health of the tooth root and surrounding bone.

If recementation is deemed appropriate, the process is generally straightforward:

  1. Cleaning the crown: The old cement residue is carefully removed from the inside of the crown.
  2. Preparing the tooth: The tooth surface is cleaned and dried to ensure optimal bonding.
  3. Checking the fit: The dentist will trial-place the crown to confirm it seats correctly before cementing.
  4. Applying new cement: A suitable dental cement is applied, and the crown is carefully positioned and held in place while the material sets.
  5. Bite adjustment: The dentist will check your bite to ensure the crown sits correctly in relation to opposing and adjacent teeth.

The appointment is typically carried out under local anaesthetic if the tooth is sensitive or if any preparation work is required. Most patients find the procedure comfortable and relatively quick.

The Dental Science Behind Crown Bonding

To understand why crowns can come loose, it helps to appreciate how they are retained in the first place.

A dental crown — whether made from porcelain, ceramic, metal, or a combination — is cemented onto a prepared tooth using specialist dental luting cement. This cement works in two primary ways: by filling the microscopic gaps between the crown and the tooth surface, and by creating a mechanical and sometimes chemical bond that holds the restoration firmly in position.

Modern dental cements include materials such as glass ionomer cement, resin-modified glass ionomer, zinc phosphate, and resin cement. Each has different properties, and the choice of cement depends on factors such as the crown material, the amount of remaining tooth structure, and whether the tooth is vital (alive) or non-vital (root-treated).

Over time, cement can be subjected to hydrolytic breakdown — essentially the gradual weakening of the bond due to moisture exposure, thermal changes in the mouth, and mechanical stress. When this occurs, micro-gaps can form at the crown margin, allowing saliva and bacteria to infiltrate. This not only risks undermining the cement bond further but can also lead to secondary decay beneath the crown if left unaddressed.

This is why regular dental check-ups are valuable — they allow your dentist to monitor the condition of existing restorations before problems escalate.

What to Do If Your Crown Falls Out: Immediate Guidance

If your crown comes out unexpectedly, it is understandable to feel concerned. The following guidance may help in the short term, but it does not replace prompt professional dental assessment.

If you are also noticing that another restoration feels uneven rather than fully detached, this guide on why a veneer may feel slightly raised may also be useful background reading.

Do:

  • Keep the crown safe — store it in a small, clean container
  • Gently clean the exposed tooth with a soft toothbrush and water
  • Contact your dental practice to arrange an appointment as soon as possible
  • Consider using a temporary dental cement (available from pharmacies) as a short-term measure if you cannot be seen immediately — always follow the product instructions carefully

Do not:

  • Attempt to glue the crown back in place using household adhesives such as super glue — these are not safe for use in the mouth and can damage both the crown and the tooth
  • Leave the exposed tooth unprotected for longer than necessary
  • Ignore the situation, even if you are not experiencing pain

It is worth noting that a tooth with a missing crown may be more sensitive to temperature changes and pressure, and the exposed tooth structure can be more vulnerable to further damage if left untreated.

When Professional Dental Assessment Is Important

There are certain situations in which arranging a dental appointment promptly is particularly advisable. Whilst a loose or fallen crown is not always a dental emergency, some accompanying symptoms may warrant more urgent attention.

Consider contacting your dentist sooner if you experience:

  • Significant pain or sensitivity in the affected tooth or surrounding area
  • Swelling of the gum near the affected tooth
  • Signs that may suggest an underlying infection, such as a persistent dull ache, a bad taste, or swelling
  • Visible damage to the tooth beneath where the crown was seated
  • Difficulty biting or chewing that is affecting your daily comfort

It is also advisable to seek professional advice promptly if the crown has been missing for several days, as teeth can shift slightly without the structural support of the crown, which may affect how well it can be replaced.

Prevention and Oral Health: Helping Your Crowns Last Longer

Whilst it is not always possible to prevent a crown from eventually requiring attention, there are practical steps patients can take to support the longevity of their dental restorations.

Maintain excellent oral hygiene: Careful brushing twice daily with a fluoride toothpaste, combined with daily interdental cleaning using floss or interdental brushes, helps protect the margins around crowns and reduces the risk of decay developing beneath them.

Attend regular dental check-ups: Routine appointments allow your dentist to monitor the condition of existing restorations and identify early signs of cement failure or wear before they become more significant issues.

Wear a nightguard if recommended: If you are prone to grinding or clenching your teeth during sleep, a custom-fitted nightguard can significantly reduce the mechanical stress placed on crowns and other restorations.

Be mindful of very hard or sticky foods: Chewing ice, hard sweets, or very chewy foods can place excessive force on crowns and may loosen them over time. Being mindful of dietary habits can help reduce unnecessary wear.

Manage acid exposure: Frequent consumption of acidic foods and beverages can weaken dental cement gradually. Rinsing with water after acidic drinks and waiting before brushing may help protect both your natural teeth and your restorations.

Key Points to Remember

  • A dental crown can often be recemented, but suitability depends on the condition of both the crown and the underlying tooth — always seek a clinical assessment.
  • Common causes of crown loosening include cement failure, decay beneath the crown, bruxism, and the natural ageing of the restoration.
  • Do not use household glue to reattach a crown — temporary dental cement from a pharmacy is a safer interim option.
  • Keep the crown safe and contact your dentist promptly after a crown comes loose or falls out.
  • Regular check-ups help identify early signs of crown wear or cement degradation before the crown becomes dislodged.
  • Good oral hygiene, dietary habits, and nightguard use (where appropriate) all contribute to the longevity of dental crowns.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take to recemented a dental crown?

The recementation procedure itself is typically completed within a single appointment and usually takes between 30 and 60 minutes, depending on the clinical assessment required. Your dentist will first examine the crown and tooth, take any necessary X-rays, and then clean and reattach the crown if appropriate. In most cases, the process is straightforward and comfortable, though your dentist will advise on what to expect based on your individual situation.

Is recementation of a crown painful?

Recementation is generally not a painful procedure. If the exposed tooth is sensitive, your dentist may use a local anaesthetic to ensure your comfort. The cleaning and reattachment process itself involves minimal discomfort for most patients. You may notice some sensitivity in the days following the appointment, particularly if the tooth had been exposed for a period of time, but this usually settles as the area stabilises.

How much does it cost to recemented a dental crown in London?

The cost of recementation varies depending on the dental practice, the complexity of the appointment, and whether additional investigations such as X-rays are required. It is generally less costly than having a new crown made. Your dental practice will be able to provide a treatment cost following assessment. It is always advisable to contact the practice directly for up-to-date pricing information specific to your clinical needs.

What happens if I leave a loose crown and do not get it treated?

Leaving a loose or fallen crown unaddressed can lead to further complications. Without the protection of the crown, the underlying tooth is more vulnerable to decay, sensitivity, and structural damage. In some cases, bacteria can work their way under a loose crown, causing decay to progress beneath it. Early professional attention may support simpler treatment outcomes compared to delaying care, though this will depend on individual clinical circumstances.

Can a crown be recemented multiple times?

In some situations, a crown may be recemented more than once over its lifespan. However, if a crown requires repeated recementation over a short period, this may indicate an underlying issue — such as an ill-fitting crown, ongoing decay, or bruxism — that needs to be addressed. Your dentist will advise on whether repeated recementation is clinically appropriate or whether a new restoration may be a more suitable long-term solution.

How can I tell if my crown is loose before it falls out?

Some patients notice early warning signs that a crown may be loosening. These can include a slight rocking or movement sensation when biting, a change in how the crown feels in the mouth, increased sensitivity to temperature or pressure, or an unusual taste around the affected tooth. If you notice any of these signs, it is advisable to contact your dentist to have the crown checked, even if it has not yet come off completely.

Conclusion

A loose or dislodged dental crown is a relatively common experience, and in many cases, recementation is a clinically viable and straightforward solution. Whether your crown has come off suddenly or you have noticed it becoming gradually loose, understanding the reasons why this can happen — and knowing what steps to take — can help you feel more confident and prepared.

The most important action is to seek professional dental advice promptly. A dentist can assess the condition of both the crown and the underlying tooth, determine whether recementation is appropriate, and guide you through the available options. Attempting to manage the situation at home beyond temporary measures is not advisable and may lead to further complications.

Supporting your oral health through regular check-ups, good hygiene habits, and appropriate protective measures such as nightguards can all contribute to the longevity of your dental restorations. A well-maintained crown may serve you for many years, depending on individual clinical factors, and attending promptly when concerns arise is generally advisable.

Dental symptoms and treatment options should always be assessed individually during a clinical examination.
