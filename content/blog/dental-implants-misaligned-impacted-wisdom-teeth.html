Introduction

Many adults considering dental implants find themselves wondering whether existing dental issues — such as misaligned or impacted wisdom teeth — might prevent them from going ahead with treatment. It is a common and entirely understandable concern. You may be dealing with one dental problem and hoping to address another simultaneously, only to find yourself uncertain about how these two issues interact.

Dental implants are a well-researched tooth replacement option available in modern dentistry, but they are not suitable for every patient without prior assessment. Where impacted or misaligned wisdom teeth are present, the situation becomes more clinically nuanced. These third molars can affect surrounding bone, gum tissue, and adjacent teeth in ways that may have a direct bearing on implant candidacy.

This article explains what impacted and misaligned wisdom teeth are, how they may relate to dental implant planning, and the factors a dental professional will typically consider before recommending a course of treatment. If you have concerns about either of these conditions, a thorough clinical assessment is always the most appropriate starting point.

Can You Get Dental Implants If You Have Misaligned or Impacted Wisdom Teeth?

Dental implants may still be possible if you have misaligned or impacted wisdom teeth, but suitability depends on individual clinical assessment. Impacted wisdom teeth can affect bone quality, gum health, and surrounding tooth structure — all of which are relevant to implant planning. A dentist will evaluate whether wisdom teeth require removal before implant treatment can proceed.

What Are Impacted and Misaligned Wisdom Teeth?

Wisdom teeth — formally known as third molars — typically emerge between the late teens and mid-twenties, though they can appear later or remain unerupted throughout a person's life. When there is insufficient space in the jaw for these teeth to emerge properly, they are described as impacted.

An impacted wisdom tooth may be partially erupted (breaking through the gum surface only partially) or fully submerged beneath the gumline. Misaligned wisdom teeth, meanwhile, may erupt at an angle — growing sideways towards adjacent molars, tilting forwards, or pushing outward towards the cheek.

Both impacted and misaligned wisdom teeth can cause a range of complications, including:

  • Pressure or crowding against adjacent teeth
  • Localised gum inflammation (pericoronitis)
  • Difficulty cleaning the area, increasing the risk of decay
  • Development of cysts around the unerupted tooth
  • Gradual resorption or damage to neighbouring tooth roots

Not every impacted or misaligned wisdom tooth causes problems. Some remain stable and asymptomatic throughout a patient's lifetime. However, when dental implant treatment is being planned, the presence of these teeth becomes a factor that requires careful evaluation.

How Might Wisdom Teeth Affect Dental Implant Planning?

Dental implants require a stable, healthy foundation in the jawbone to integrate successfully — a biological process known as osseointegration. When impacted or misaligned wisdom teeth are present, several factors may need to be addressed before implant placement is appropriate.

Bone quality and volume are central to implant success. Impacted wisdom teeth, particularly those associated with cysts or chronic infection, can cause localised bone loss. If the implant site is in or near an affected area, bone density and volume may be insufficient to support an implant without additional procedures such as bone grafting.

Gum health is equally important. Active infection, inflammation, or periodontal disease — which impacted wisdom teeth can contribute to — must typically be managed before implant surgery is considered. Placing an implant in a compromised environment increases the risk of implant failure.

Proximity of the wisdom tooth to the implant site also matters. If the wisdom tooth sits adjacent to or encroaches on the planned implant location, its presence may physically obstruct placement or affect the long-term stability of the implant.

In cases where a wisdom tooth has caused damage to an adjacent molar (the tooth being replaced by an implant), understanding the extent of that damage is critical to treatment planning. The Clinical Explanation: Bone, Tissue, and Osseointegration

To understand why wisdom teeth matter in implant planning, it helps to appreciate a little of the underlying dental science.

Dental implants are titanium posts placed surgically into the jawbone. Over a period of several weeks to months, the bone tissue grows around and bonds to the surface of the implant — a process called osseointegration. For this to succeed reliably, the bone at the implant site needs to meet certain criteria in terms of density, volume, and health.

The jawbone is a living tissue that responds dynamically to the teeth and forces placed upon it. When a tooth is lost, the bone in that area begins to resorb (diminish) over time because it is no longer receiving the stimulation it needs. Impacted wisdom teeth can compound this process in two ways. Firstly, any cyst or chronic infection associated with an impacted tooth can directly destroy surrounding bone tissue. Secondly, impacted teeth can cause pressure-related resorption of adjacent tooth roots, which in turn affects the bone around those roots.

Gum tissue, too, plays a role. The gum must be healthy and well-attached around an implant for long-term stability. Impacted wisdom teeth are a known contributor to a localised gum infection called pericoronitis — where bacteria accumulate beneath the flap of gum tissue overlying a partially erupted tooth. Left unmanaged, this can progress to more widespread gum disease.

Understanding these biological processes helps explain why a dentist will conduct a thorough assessment — including dental X-rays or cone beam CT (CBCT) imaging — before forming any treatment recommendation.

Does the Wisdom Tooth Always Need to Be Removed First?

This is one of the most common questions patients ask, and the honest answer is: it depends on the clinical picture. There is no universal rule that impacted or misaligned wisdom teeth must be extracted before dental implants can be placed.

In some cases, a wisdom tooth that is fully impacted, stable, showing no signs of infection or cyst formation, and not adjacent to the implant site may be left in place without compromising implant treatment. The decision is highly individual and rests on a thorough examination and imaging assessment.

However, wisdom tooth extraction prior to implant placement is commonly recommended when:

  • There is evidence of infection, cyst, or bone loss associated with the wisdom tooth
  • The wisdom tooth is damaging or likely to damage the implant site
  • Active gum disease linked to the wisdom tooth is present
  • The wisdom tooth is causing ongoing pain or crowding that complicates treatment planning
  • The wisdom tooth's position would physically prevent implant placement

If extraction is required, a period of healing is generally advised before implant surgery proceeds, allowing the bone and gum tissue to recover fully. Your dentist will advise on the most appropriate sequencing of treatment based on your specific circumstances. When Professional Dental Assessment May Be Appropriate

If you are considering dental implants and are aware that you have impacted or misaligned wisdom teeth, arranging a comprehensive dental consultation is an important first step. You do not need to be in pain for an assessment to be worthwhile — early evaluation may provide more options and support more informed treatment planning.

There are, however, certain situations where dental assessment may be particularly important to seek promptly:

  • Persistent pain or aching around the back of the jaw or adjacent teeth
  • Swelling, redness, or warmth in the gum tissue near a wisdom tooth
  • Difficulty opening the mouth fully (trismus) or pain when chewing
  • Unpleasant taste or odour in the mouth, which can indicate infection
  • Sensitivity or pain in the tooth adjacent to a wisdom tooth
  • Visible decay on a partially erupted wisdom tooth or neighbouring molar

None of these symptoms should cause alarm, but each may indicate that a wisdom tooth is having an effect on the surrounding structures that warrants attention. A calm, thorough conversation with a dental professional is always the most effective way to understand what, if anything, needs to be done.

The Role of Dental Imaging in Treatment Planning

Modern dental imaging plays an essential role in understanding the relationship between wisdom teeth and potential implant sites. A standard dental X-ray (periapical or panoramic radiograph) provides a useful overview, but cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) — a three-dimensional imaging technique — allows a much more detailed assessment of bone volume, root proximity, nerve position, and any pathology associated with impacted teeth.

CBCT imaging is increasingly used in implant planning because it allows the dentist to visualise the implant site in three dimensions before any surgery takes place. This precision reduces guesswork and helps ensure that implant placement is safe and appropriately positioned.

This multidisciplinary approach is well established in UK dental care and is something you should feel reassured by, not concerned about.

Prevention and Oral Health Advice

While not all impacted wisdom teeth can be prevented — their development is largely determined by jaw size and genetics — there are meaningful steps you can take to protect your oral health and reduce the risk of complications.

Maintain regular dental appointments. Routine check-ups allow your dentist to monitor developing wisdom teeth using X-rays, often identifying potential problems before they cause symptoms. The British Dental Association recommends attendance frequency based on individual risk, so follow your dentist's personalised guidance.

Clean the back of your mouth thoroughly. The area around partially erupted wisdom teeth is notoriously difficult to clean. Using a small-headed toothbrush, interdental brushes, and an antibacterial mouthwash can help reduce the bacterial load in this area.

Do not ignore early symptoms. Mild discomfort or occasional soreness around a wisdom tooth is worth mentioning at your next appointment. Early intervention is typically simpler and less disruptive than managing established complications.

Discuss implant planning early. If you are aware of missing teeth or teeth likely to require extraction, raising the question of dental implants early in the process allows your dentist to plan treatment in the most logical and effective sequence.

Avoid smoking. Smoking significantly impairs healing and increases the risk of implant failure, as well as contributing to gum disease. Patients who smoke and are considering implants should discuss this with their dentist openly.

For patients managing multiple dental concerns simultaneously, a full-mouth assessment can help establish the most appropriate treatment priority and sequence.

Key Points to Remember

  • Dental implants may still be possible even if you have impacted or misaligned wisdom teeth, but this depends entirely on individual clinical assessment.
  • Bone quality, gum health, and wisdom tooth position are among the key factors a dentist will evaluate when planning implant treatment.
  • Wisdom tooth extraction is not always required before implants, but it is frequently recommended when infection, bone loss, or proximity to the implant site is a concern.
  • CBCT imaging provides a detailed three-dimensional view of the jaw, helping dental teams plan treatment more precisely and safely.
  • Regular dental check-ups allow wisdom teeth to be monitored over time, reducing the likelihood of complications arising without warning.
  • Early consultation is always beneficial — understanding your individual situation sooner rather than later may support more informed treatment decisions.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can impacted wisdom teeth cause dental implants to fail?

Impacted wisdom teeth do not directly cause implant failure, but the complications they produce — such as localised bone loss, infection, or gum disease — can create an environment where implant success is compromised. If an impacted wisdom tooth has damaged the bone in the area where an implant is planned, additional procedures such as bone grafting may be required before implant placement can proceed safely. Your dentist will assess the specific situation and advise accordingly.

How long after wisdom tooth removal can I get a dental implant?

There is no single answer to this question, as healing times vary between individuals and depend on the complexity of the extraction, the amount of bone involved, and whether any bone loss or infection was present. In straightforward cases, a dentist may recommend waiting approximately three to six months for adequate healing before implant placement. In cases involving bone grafting or significant tissue repair, longer periods may be required. Your dentist will provide a personalised timeline following assessment.

Will my dentist always know if I have impacted wisdom teeth?

Impacted wisdom teeth are not always visible during a routine oral examination, particularly if they are fully submerged beneath the gumline. Dental X-rays are essential for identifying impacted teeth and assessing their position, angle, and proximity to adjacent structures. This is one reason why regular dental check-ups that include periodic radiographic assessment are important, especially during the years when wisdom teeth typically develop.

Can misaligned wisdom teeth damage the teeth next to them?

Yes, misaligned wisdom teeth — particularly those growing at an angle towards adjacent molars — can place pressure on neighbouring teeth, potentially contributing to root resorption (where the root of the adjacent tooth gradually breaks down), decay in hard-to-clean areas, and structural damage. The extent of any damage depends on the angle of the wisdom tooth, the duration of the problem, and how promptly it is identified and managed.

Is it possible to have dental implants placed at the same time as wisdom tooth extraction?

In certain carefully selected cases, it may be possible to extract a wisdom tooth and place a dental implant in the same surgical session. However, this approach depends on several clinical factors, including the condition of the bone, the absence of active infection, and the proximity of the two sites. It is not appropriate in all circumstances and requires careful pre-operative planning. Your dental team will advise on whether same-session treatment is suitable in your case.

What happens if I choose not to treat impacted wisdom teeth before getting implants?

Choosing to proceed with implant treatment without addressing problematic impacted wisdom teeth carries a potential risk of ongoing or worsening complications at the implant site. If the impacted tooth is associated with infection or bone loss, these issues could affect the long-term stability of the implant. That said, not all impacted wisdom teeth require treatment. A dentist will assess the risk on a case-by-case basis and will explain the reasoning behind any recommendation clearly.

Conclusion

The relationship between impacted or misaligned wisdom teeth and dental implant candidacy is genuinely complex, and it is a topic that deserves careful, individualised consideration rather than generalised assumptions. In many cases, dental implants remain a viable treatment option even where wisdom teeth are present — but the specifics matter enormously.

Bone health, gum condition, the position of the wisdom tooth relative to the planned implant site, and the presence of any associated infection or pathology are all factors that must be evaluated by a qualified dental professional before any treatment plan is formed. Imaging plays a key role in making this assessment accurately.

What is clear is that dental implants — when placed in appropriately selected patients, following thorough planning — represent a well-evidenced approach to tooth replacement. Where wisdom teeth are part of the clinical picture, addressing them as part of a coordinated treatment plan is usually the most effective path forward.
